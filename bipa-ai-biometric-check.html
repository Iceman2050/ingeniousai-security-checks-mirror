Why AI companies keep getting caught

  • Biometrics are the product, not a side effect. Face filters, avatars, voice cloning, and transcription all derive biometric identifiers by design — you can't "not notice" you're collecting them.
  • A selfie counts. The Grok suits allege a single uploaded selfie triggered collection. BIPA covers face geometry derived from a photo, not just a fingerprint scanner.
  • No injury needed. Since Rosenbach (2019), plaintiffs need only show the technical violation — no hacked database, no leaked data, no harm.
  • 900+ class actions filed. BIPA is the only US biometric law with a private right of action, which built a plaintiffs'-bar industry around it.
  • It follows the user. Out-of-state and foreign companies get sued. "We're not in Illinois" is not a defense if Illinois residents use your product.
  • The wave is accelerating. Paul Hastings warned in April 2026 of "a new wave of biometrics litigation" aimed squarely at AI companies.

What to do right now

  • Add a written biometric consent step before first collection — naming what you collect, why, and for how long. Not a ToS checkbox: a standalone, logged release.
  • Publish a retention & destruction schedule and actually delete on schedule — §15(a) requires both the policy and the practice.
  • Map every third party that touches biometric data (cloud vision APIs, analytics, subprocessors) and get consent covering disclosure — or stop the flow.
  • Minimize: if you only need a result (e.g. "face detected"), don't store the template. Less stored = less exposure.
  • Decide Illinois deliberately: either geo-exclude and document it, or comply fully. "We didn't think about it" is the fact pattern in every complaint.
