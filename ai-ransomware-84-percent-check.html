Cybersecurity News: AI Is Now Used in 84% of Ransomware Attacks

This week's cybersecurity news is a ransomware report that reads like an escalation ladder. Semperis' 2026 Ransomware Risk Report — built with Censuswide from 1,200 IT and security professionals across 9 countries, every one of them hit by ransomware in the past year — finds that attackers weaponized AI in 84% of attacks. And the numbers keep getting darker from there: 87% of attacks targeted identity systems, 44% targeted the victims' own AI systems, and 40% included threats against employees and their families.

What is the Semperis 2026 Ransomware Risk Report?

Released around October 14, 2026, the report surveyed 1,200 IT and security professionals in the US, UK, France, Germany, Italy, Spain, Singapore, Canada, and Australia, spanning 8 industry sectors. All respondents' organizations had been hit by ransomware in the preceding 12 months. The key figures:

84%attacks used weaponized AI
87%targeted identity systems
44%targeted victims' own AI systems
69%suffered multiple breaches in a year
59%paid a ransom at least once
31%paid multiple ransoms

Sources: Semperis 2026 Ransomware Risk Report; CBS News (Inglis interview); policy-wire.com; inview.org.uk; thearabianpost.com — October 2026.

Honest scope: this is a VENDOR survey — Semperis sells identity resilience — of self-reported victim experiences, not measured prevalence across all businesses. The figures rest on participants' accounts, not independently documented cases. Treat the direction as signal, the decimals as survey data.

How did ransomware tactics evolve to this point?

Semperis CEO Mickey Bresman describes a three-stage escalation: first came encryption (pay or lose your data), then double extortion (encryption plus threats to leak stolen data), and now personal and family pressure. When 40% of attacks include psychological or physical threats against employees and their families — with 53% of intimidated victims saying attackers followed through, 37% reporting threats against relatives or friends, and 34% of victims or families being swatted — the ransom demand has moved beyond the server room and into the home.

Why does this cybersecurity news change defense plans?

Former US National Cyber Director Chris Inglis, now a Semperis strategic adviser, put it bluntly: "I think we're already seeing autonomous attacks." AI lets low-skill actors run sophisticated breaches — automating reconnaissance, crafting convincing lures, adapting to defenses in real time. And in a bitter irony, 44% of organizations reported attacks specifically targeting their own internal AI systems: the models and agents deployed to help the business have become attack surfaces of their own. "Holding you at physical risk is the latest phenomenon because we've covered most of the other risks," Inglis added — explaining why the 27% who sought psychological or medical treatment, the 24% who hired personal security, and the 14% who relocated or changed jobs are part of this story too.
