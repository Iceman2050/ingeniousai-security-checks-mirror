Cybersecurity 2026: What 10 Security Vendors Say Comes Next

A reader's guide to The Hacker News report Cybersecurity 2026: Executive Insights from Leading Companies Shaping the Future of Cybersecurity — plus a 5-question readiness check for your org.

What this report actually is

The report (distributed via The Hacker News, hosted as a flipbook) dedicates one chapter to each of ten cybersecurity companies. Every chapter pairs a trend overview with that company's own perspective — an executive quote, a product description, and a contact card. That makes this vendor-sourced content in a sponsored format, not independent research: each company is describing the future through the lens of what it sells.

We read the full 24-page report so you don't have to. Below, every company's angle is paraphrased as their claim — not as verified fact. Their statistics are their own; we did not independently verify them. The quiz at the bottom tests whether your org is actually positioned for the shifts they describe.

The 10 companies, in one line each

1. Keeper Security — Identity Security

CEO Darren Guccione's angle

Keeper says identity is the new security perimeter, and that perimeter now includes non-human identities: service accounts, API keys, cloud workloads, and AI agents. Its pitch is KeeperPAM — password, secrets, session, and privilege management plus zero-trust network access in one console — built on a zero-knowledge architecture, with just-in-time access replacing standing privilege. Keeper claims its research found 96% of organizations say poorly integrated tools are creating gaps attackers exploit.

2. Cribl — Telemetry & Data Management

Senior Director Nicole Beckwith's angle

Cribl says security teams don't have a data problem, they have a "data control problem": the winning programs won't be the ones ingesting the most data, but the ones that can route, reshape, and reuse it on demand. Its pitch is a vendor-agnostic telemetry pipeline — filter, normalize, enrich, and tier data before it reaches expensive tools — plus an AI observability app to spot shadow AI and unapproved MCP servers.

3. Automox — Endpoint Management

CEO Justin Talerico's angle

Automox's mantra: patch what can be patched, mitigate what can't, and govern the endpoint continuously. It says the window between vulnerability disclosure and exploitation keeps shrinking, so endpoint management must move from periodic maintenance to always-on, policy-driven automation — including 630+ third-party apps — with "governed autonomy" keeping AI-assisted actions under human oversight.

4. Nisos — Human Risk Intelligence

CEO Ryan LaSalle's angle

Nisos says human risk is now a board-level issue: executives, employees, candidates, and third parties. Its pitch combines analyst-led investigations with the Ascend platform — including "Identity Integrity" checks that cross-reference a candidate's claims against their wider digital footprint. Its headline evidence: research into a North Korean remote-worker scheme that it says linked 166,000+ job applications and 76 job offers to one cell.

5. Surf AI — Exposure Management

Co-founder & CEO Yair Grindlinger's angle

Surf says "discovery is commoditized. The middle is hard": finding vulnerabilities is solved; the hard part is understanding what an exposure means, who owns it, and how to remediate it safely. Its pitch is a per-tenant "Context Graph" of each customer's environment feeding agentic remediation workflows. Grindlinger cites Verizon's 2026 DBIR: vulnerability exploitation is the top attack vector for the first time in 20 years.

6. Adaptive Security — Human Security

Co-founder & CPO Andrew Jones's angle

Adaptive says traditional annual awareness training wasn't built for AI-powered social engineering, and its mission is "to protect people from AI-powered cyberattacks." Its pitch: personalized deepfake and phishing simulations built around each company's own org chart and public footprint — train people at the moment they fail. It claims one credit union cut its phishing failure rate by 86% over six months.

7. Red Sift — Email & Domain Security

Co-founder & CEO Rahul Powar's angle

Red Sift says email impersonation is no longer an inbox problem but an infrastructure problem spanning email, domains, DNS, and certificates — each link in the chain a trust decision made in public infrastructure. Its pitch combines OnDMARC, DNS monitoring, and lookalike-domain brand protection. It says it monitors 60M+ domains and logs 113M fraudulent messages a day — and calls today's AI "the worst AI is ever going to be."

8. Asimily — Connected Device Security

CEO Shankar Somasundaram's angle

Asimily says "identification is the input, not the finished job": knowing a device is at risk must end in an enforced control. Its pitch starts with agentless inventory of IoT, OT, medical, and IT devices, prioritizes by genuine exploitability rather than raw severity, then generates and simulates segmentation policies in the customer's existing NAC format before applying them.

9. SentinelOne — AI-Native Security Operations

Area VP Paolo Cecchi's angle

SentinelOne says SOC analysts can't investigate at the speed attackers now operate, so AI must close the gap: its Purple AI "Asimov" agent auto-investigates incidents and assembles context for analysts. Its stated line: AI should accelerate, support, and suggest — but not replace human judgment. It also flags shadow AI — employee-adopted AI tools security never approved — as a growing visibility gap.

10. CrowdStrike — Cloud Security

VP Kartik Shahani's angle

CrowdStrike says attackers have shifted from breaking in to logging in: citing its 2026 Global Threat Report, 82% of detections are now malware-free and cloud intrusions rose 37% in 2025. Its pitch is real-time cloud detection and response plus AI workload protection — runtime inspection of prompts, responses, and agent actions against prompt injection and data leaks — inside the Falcon platform.

The real trends underneath the marketing

  • Identity is the perimeter — and it now includes machines, service accounts, and AI agents, not just employees. Least-privilege and just-in-time access are becoming the enterprise standard.
  • Discovery is commoditized; remediation with context is the bottleneck. The question isn't "how many vulns" but which exposures are reachable, who owns them, and what can safely change.
  • Telemetry control beats telemetry volume. Routing, filtering, and tiering data before it hits expensive tools is becoming as important as collecting it.
  • Email and domain impersonation is an infrastructure problem spanning domains, DNS, and certificates — not just the inbox.
  • AI has industrialized deception. Voice cloning, deepfakes, and synthetic identities mean verification processes matter more than training alone.
  • The SOC speed gap. AI investigates at machine speed; humans stay in command of the decisions that matter.
  • Continuous verification replaces static controls. Across every chapter, the direction is the same: trust continuously established, not assumed once.

Readiness check: is your org positioned for this shift?

5 questions. Answer honestly — this is for you, not for a vendor.

1. Does your org inventory and govern non-human identities (service accounts, API keys, AI agents) with the same rigor as employee accounts?

2. When vulnerabilities surface, do you prioritize by exploitability and business impact rather than raw severity counts?

3. Do you ship all telemetry to a single SIEM without filtering, tiering, or routing it first?

4. Could your team contain an exposed IoT or OT device with an enforced control (e.g. network quarantine) when no vendor patch exists?

5. Does everyone in your org get the same annual security awareness training regardless of their role?
