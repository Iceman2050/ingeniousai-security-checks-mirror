What the stories actually show (verified)

1. The face match that jailed the wrong woman

  • Angela Robin Lipps, 50, a Tennessee grandmother, was arrested by U.S. Marshals on July 14, 2025 while babysitting in Elizabethton, TN — on a warrant from Fargo, North Dakota, a state she says she never visited.
  • Investigators ran a photo from a fake ID used in Fargo-area bank frauds through Clearview AI; the search returned her social media photo as a possible match.
  • She spent 108 days in a Tennessee jail plus two more months in a North Dakota maximum-security facility on a $100,000 bond.
  • All eight felony charges were dismissed on December 24, 2025 — her release day — after bank records proved she was in Tennessee during the thefts.
  • On September 15, 2026 she sued Fargo and Detective Lucas Heck for $10 million, alleging false arrest and malicious prosecution.
  • The critical nuance: face matches are supposed to be investigative leads, not identifications. West Fargo reportedly didn't consider the match strong enough to charge — Fargo's detective allegedly never verified it, never disclosed it came from a fake ID, and ignored obvious physical differences (build, a distinctive neck tattoo).

2. Target's basket beacons — real tech, exaggerated claims

  • A Target employee's viral TikTok (Sept 2026, 3.4M views) pointed at a small device on store baskets, claiming shoppers were being tracked. Reported by the NY Post.
  • Verified: the device is a battery-powered Bluetooth Low Energy beacon — part of Target's indoor-positioning system (Acuity Brands LED partnership dating to ~2017).
  • The honest correction: the beacon reports the basket's location — it holds no camera, microphone, or personal identifier. The viral "tracking YOU" framing is unverified.
  • But: combine basket location with the Target app, your account, and your transaction history, and the retailer can build a far more complete picture. Target's privacy policy explicitly admits to in-store location collection via Bluetooth signals and LED-chip tech.

3. Instacart's Caper Carts — the rolling data rig

  • AI smart carts with cameras, scales, and location tracking that scan items as you shop and check out from the cart.
  • Verified rollout: thousands of carts across 100+ U.S. cities and 15 states (per Instacart, May 2026), at Kroger, ShopRite, Fairway Market, Weis Markets and more.
  • The cart logs every item in/out, your precise location, loyalty-linked purchases, and your responses to location-based deal nudges — merged with your online Instacart history.
  • The concern: "surveillance pricing" — personalized deals priced off what your data suggests you'll pay. A cited study found a 32% difference in average spending with smart carts (context: the study was German, not U.S.).

4. McDonald's AI drive-thru — what actually happened

  • McDonald's tested IBM's Automated Order Taking at 100+ U.S. restaurants and switched it off by July 26, 2024 after accuracy problems — viral clips showed hundreds of dollars of unwanted chicken nuggets added to orders.
  • Not verified: the viral claim of "2 million" humans hired back. It was a pilot at ~100 locations, never a replacement of drive-thru staff.
  • In 2026 McDonald's began testing a new Google-powered system ("Archy") at a handful of restaurants — AI ordering isn't dead, it's on version two.

Three defenses that actually work

1. Lock down retail app permissions (2 minutes)

Android: Settings → Apps → [store app] → Permissions → Location → Don't allow. iPhone: Settings → Privacy & Security → Location Services → [store app] → Never.

2. Audit your public face photos

Clearview-type databases are built from scraped social media images. Public profile photos are the raw material — set albums to friends-only and strip face tags.

3. Read biometric fine print before you hand over your ID

Dealerships and rental counters increasingly scan IDs with fine print allowing biometric face-printing and third-party sharing. Ask what the scan is for, and whether you can opt out.
