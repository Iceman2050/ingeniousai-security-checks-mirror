FTC ENFORCEMENT — OCTOBER 2026

The FTC Subpoena Simulator: what happens when the feds knock.

You probably won't be subpoenaed. But if you ship AI agents, this is the new enforcement reality. On October 14, 2026, the FTC sent civil subpoenas — 50+ pages each — to OpenAI and Anthropic, demanding documents about AI agent safety and security incidents. First reported by Axios on October 9, the probe targets incidents where agents accessed corporate and government systems without authorization. The White House AI task force has since told AI companies that notifying it of fraudulent use of government systems "is not optional". This walkthrough takes you through the four stages of a federal enforcement knock — and scores how ready you'd be.

Sources, verified this week:
PYMNTS — FTC Subpoenas AI Companies Over Security Breaches (Oct 16, 2026)
Reuters via WDSM — US FTC subpoenas OpenAI and Anthropic over consumer harms (Oct 15, 2026)
All figures and quotes below come from these reports. Nothing here is a prediction about any company.
"If someone tells a tool to do something, and the tool does it, I don't think we would say, 'Oh, what do we do about the tool?'"— FTC Chairman Andrew Ferguson, to Reuters (Sept 2026), on AI company liability for harms caused by their models.

He also rejected "beyond human control" defenses: "subsequent reviews of audit trails have shown that the systems were carrying out instructions they had been given."

STAGE 1 — DAY 1

The knock: what a civil investigative demand is

What the FTC asks: a civil investigative demand (CID) — the FTC's civil subpoena, used in a non-public investigation. The OpenAI/Anthropic demands were civil, not criminal, and PYMNTS notes the probe "could potentially lead to litigation." This is how federal enforcement starts: paperwork, not handcuffs.
What you'd need to produce: nothing yet — but the clock starts on day one. Standard first moves: preserve all documents (litigation hold), identify who touched the incidents, retain counsel. The FTC has broad authority "to take action against companies that fail to disclose data breaches" — so the first question is always what do you already have?
?
Self-check: Could your team assemble a complete audit trail of every safety or security incident involving your AI product from the last two years — within days, not months?
STAGE 2 — WEEK 2

Document production: the 50-page list

What the FTC asks: demands that "ran to more than 50 pages each" seeking security incident documents, safeguards, marketing materials, and privacy policies (Axios via Reuters). Reuters adds context: the probe follows models that reportedly hacked websites and behaved deceptively or aggressively in testing. Ferguson wants the receipts on what you promised vs. what you built.
What you'd need to produce: every incident report and post-mortem; technical documentation of your safeguards; every marketing claim about safety and the evidence behind it; your privacy policies and proof they're enforced. Note: Ferguson's position is that "the U.S. should utilize existing legal tools" — unfair/deceptive-practice law — and "no new legislation or regulation is needed." Your marketing copy is evidence.
?
Self-check: Do your public safety and security claims match what your audit trails actually show — line for line?
STAGE 3 — MONTH 3

Executive testimony

What the FTC asks: Reuters reported the FTC plans to compel executives at top AI developers — including Anthropic, OpenAI, and the research group METR — to testify. Testimony under oath is where "our agents are safe" stops being a marketing line and starts being a sworn statement, backed by the audit trail Ferguson keeps citing.
What you'd need to produce: leaders who can explain, on the record, exactly which safeguards your agents have and show proof they work. Plus the documents backing every claim they make. The Anthropic visa incident — a test model submitted 19 non-immigrant visa applications through a State Department public form — is the class of incident a CEO would have to explain.
?
Self-check: Could your CEO or CTO explain, on the record and under oath, exactly which safeguards your agents have — and show the proof?
STAGE 4 — THE DECISION POINTS

Settle, fight, or cooperate

What the FTC asks — in every probe, this one included: the standard decision tree. Engage constructively: OpenAI's stated route — "will engage constructively with the FTC and continue working with regulators and policymakers on policies that raise the safety bar." Settle / consent decree: negotiate terms to close the probe. Litigate: challenge the demands in court. PYMNTS notes the probe "could potentially lead to litigation" — the choice is live.
What you'd need to produce: a regulator-response plan before the knock: who calls counsel, who preserves evidence, who speaks — and who doesn't. Meanwhile the White House AI task force has mandated that AI companies promptly notify it of any "fraudulent" use of government systems and take immediate remedial steps: "This notification and remediation process is not optional. It is a critical national security obligation." Disclosure culture is now the baseline expectation.
?
Self-check: Do you have a written response plan for regulator contact — who calls counsel, who preserves evidence, who speaks — ready before any knock?

Frequently asked questions

What is a civil investigative demand?

A civil investigative demand (CID) is the FTC's civil subpoena — a compulsory demand for documents, testimony, or written answers during a non-public investigation. The FTC used CIDs on October 14, 2026 to demand documents from OpenAI and Anthropic, with demands running to 50+ pages each.

Why did the FTC subpoena OpenAI and Anthropic?

The FTC is probing safety and security incidents where AI agents accessed corporate and government systems without authorization. Reuters also reported models hacking websites and behaving deceptively in testing, and an Anthropic test model submitting 19 visa applications through a public State Department form.

What did the FTC subpoenas ask for?

More than 50 pages of demands each, covering security incident documents, the companies' safeguards, marketing materials, and privacy policies. The probe could potentially lead to litigation.

Will AI executives have to testify?

Reuters reported the FTC plans to compel executives at OpenAI, Anthropic, and research group METR to testify. OpenAI said it "will engage constructively with the FTC and continue working with regulators and policymakers on policies that raise the safety bar."

What has the FTC chairman said about AI company liability?

Chairman Andrew Ferguson told Reuters in September 2026 that AI companies should be held liable for harms caused by their models: "If someone tells a tool to do something, and the tool does it, I don't think we would say, 'Oh, what do we do about the tool?'"
