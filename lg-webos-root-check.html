Can someone on your WiFi take over your LG smart TV?

Demonstrated at TyphoonPWN 2026: three flaws in LG's webOS chain together to hand an attacker on your local network root access to certain smart TVs — no PIN, no click, no interaction from you. The attack enters through the TV's own pairing service, escalates through a trusted built-in app, and finishes with a command injection in a logging service that runs as root. LG has issued a fix. This free 30-second check tells you if you're at risk. No signup, nothing installed.

The 30-second check

1

Check your model and firmware — on the TV: Settings → General → About This TV. Note the model number, the webOS version, and the software (firmware) version.

Model 32LQ636BPSA or 27TQ600SW on webOS 7.5.7-25 / firmware 04.54.40 → CONFIRMED EXPOSED. These are the exact models and firmware the attack was demonstrated on. Update immediately.
Any other LG webOS model → scope unknown. LG has not published which other models or firmware versions are affected. Treat an unpatched TV as at-risk and update anyway.
Firmware already includes LG's October 2026 patch → fixed. The patch was issued per the SSD Secure Disclosure advisory of October 12, 2026.
2

Check for a firmware update right now — Settings → General → About This TV → Check for Updates. Install anything it offers, then re-check — LG sometimes ships fixes in staged rollouts.

No update offered and your model is on the confirmed list? Check again in a few days — and until then, see step 3. There is no other workaround.
3

Check who shares your TV's network — this attack needs no internet and no phishing: any compromised device on the same local network can reach the TV's pairing service on port 3001. Ask yourself:

  • Do guests, tenants, or visitors join your main WiFi?
  • Is the TV on the same network as laptops, phones, and cheap IoT gadgets?
  • Does your router offer a guest network or IoT VLAN? If yes, move the TV onto it — a segmented TV can't be reached by an attacker's laptop on the main network.
TV on the main network with untrusted devices around → exposed attack surface even after patching, for the next flaw. Network segmentation is the durable fix.

How the chain works (so you know what you're defending)

  • Flaw 1 — PIN bypass: the TV's pairing service (port 3001, used by the LG ThinQ app) accepts a replayed manifest from the built-in sync-demo app, signed with a test certificate the firmware trusts. A permission in that manifest skips the PIN prompt and hands over a privileged key.
  • Flaw 2 — trusted app redirection: with that key, the attacker launches another built-in app, cmp-client, and points it at an attacker-controlled page. The app doesn't validate where it navigates, and the loaded page keeps access to PalmServiceBridge — the internal interface to the TV's system services.
  • Flaw 3 — root command injection: through that bridge, the attacker calls the collectAllLog method of pmlogd, the system logging service. It builds shell commands from caller-controlled input without sanitizing it. pmlogd runs as root — so the injected command runs as root.

No CVE identifiers have been assigned and no in-the-wild exploitation has been reported as of October 15, 2026. This is not the first webOS local-network root chain: Bitdefender disclosed a different set in 2024 (patched March 2024), and another chain was shown at TyphoonPWN 2025.

If you're exposed, do this now

  • Update the firmware today — this is the fix. Don't wait for the TV's auto-update schedule.
  • Segment your network: put the TV (and all IoT) on a guest network or IoT VLAN, away from laptops and phones. This shrinks the attack surface for this flaw and the next one.
  • Can't update yet? Disconnect the TV from the network when you're not actively using its smart features. There is no other workaround.
  • Don't assume one brand is safe: smart TVs run full operating systems with network services, microphones, and your streaming credentials — treat them like the computers they are.
