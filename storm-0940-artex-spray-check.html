Cybersecurity News Today: Storm-0940 Used an AI Framework to Spray Passwords at 2,900 Sign-Ins a Minute

The sharpest story in cybersecurity news today is about speed and trust, not a new flaw in any one product. On October 21, 2026, Microsoft Threat Intelligence reported that Storm-0940, a China state-aligned actor it has tracked since 2021, used ARTEX, an open-source agentic AI penetration-testing framework, to automate attacks on hundreds of organizations. The framework sprayed passwords at a peak of 2,900 sign-in attempts per minute. Where spraying failed, the actor signed in through OAuth applications that already held granted access, a route that never asks a user for a password. Several intrusions reached privileged cloud access in under three hours. This page states what Microsoft confirmed, then gives you a working check: your own sign-in numbers, rated against the incident's own benchmarks, plus a review of the application identities an attacker would rather use than yours.

Honest scope, read this first: every figure on this page comes from Microsoft's October 21, 2026 report, and the checker below compares your numbers to those figures. Nothing here detects Storm-0940 in your tenant. A spray at the incident's rate is loud and easy to spot in sign-in logs; the quieter half of this campaign is the application-identity abuse, which leaves no failed sign-ins at all. That is why the page has two checks, not one. Most small organizations will score normal on the spray check and still carry real exposure in the OAuth review.

What Storm-0940 did, as Microsoft reported it

  • The tool: ARTEX is an open-source agentic AI penetration-testing framework. Microsoft says Storm-0940 used it from as early as September 2026 to automate reconnaissance and attacks that a human team would run far more slowly.
  • The spray: password spraying peaked at 2,900 sign-in attempts per minute and reached more than 2,250 customer tenants. Between October 10 and 14, Microsoft counted more than 151,000 failed authentication attempts against 327 accounts across 8 organizations. That is about 462 attempts per account, sustained at roughly 26 attempts per minute across the four days.
  • The trusted route in: the actor authenticated through applications that already had granted access, using application identities (service principals) instead of a user sign-in. No interactive sign-in took place on that route.
  • After access: Microsoft reports reconnaissance, application credential changes, and attempts to modify privileges as the follow-on activity. Those changes are how an intruder widens access and keeps it.
  • The supply-chain link: Microsoft attributes the ConnectWise Cloud Backup supply-chain compromise, a multitenant application disclosed on October 15, to Storm-0940. One application identity there sits above many customer tenants at once.
  • Who was targeted: Microsoft lists sectors that include aerospace, defense, energy, financial services, government, manufacturing, semiconductors, and technology.

Put the spray in proportion. A person mistyping a password makes a handful of failed attempts against one account. A spray makes hundreds of attempts per account across hundreds of accounts, and nearly all of them fail. The signal is not any single failure; it is the shape: high volume, wide spread, near-total failure. The checker below measures exactly that shape in your numbers.

Why does this cybersecurity news today matter?

The identity you granted is the target. Password spraying is an old technique. What ARTEX changed is the labor: an agentic framework runs the scanning, the spraying, and the follow-on steps without a person at each keyboard, so the campaign's pace is set by infrastructure, not by staffing. Microsoft's figures show what that pace does to defenders. At 2,900 attempts per minute, a detection rule that waits for a human to review a daily report is a day late.

The second half of the campaign is the part worth sitting with. An OAuth application with granted access is an identity your organization created and trusted. It has no password to spray and no user to phish. When Storm-0940 authenticated as an application, the security controls aimed at people (password policy, sign-in risk, one-time codes) had nothing to evaluate. The controls that would have caught it are the ones Microsoft names in its guidance: watching service principal sign-ins and auditing unexpected application credential or privilege changes. If nobody in your organization can list your service principals and what each one may do, that list is the first thing to build.

What to do now, in order

  • Read your service principal sign-ins. Microsoft's published guidance for this campaign starts here: investigate service principal sign-ins and unexpected application credential or privilege changes. Pull 90 days. Any application signing in from a network or an hour you do not recognize gets named and explained, in writing.
  • Audit credential and privilege changes on applications. Storm-0940 changed application credentials and tried to modify privileges after access. Match every recent change to a named admin and a change record. A change with no owner is treated as hostile until it has one.
  • Turn on detection, not just logging. Microsoft says Defender detections exist for this activity. If you run a different stack, build the equivalent alerts: service principal sign-in from a new network, application credential added, application privilege changed.
  • Set a spray tripwire. Alert on sign-in attempt rate, not just on failures per account. The incident's sustained rate was about 26 attempts per minute against the targeted set; a tripwire at 5 attempts per minute across your tenant gives you hours of warning that a per-account lockout never will.
  • Cut standing privilege on applications. Remove high-privilege directory roles from service principals that do not need them, delete consent and credentials for applications nobody uses, and evict multitenant apps whose vendor you cannot name. Every grant you remove is a route this campaign cannot take.

Frequently asked questions

What did Storm-0940 do, in plain terms?

Storm-0940 is a China state-aligned hacking group Microsoft has tracked since 2021. In a report published October 21, 2026, Microsoft Threat Intelligence said the group used ARTEX, an open-source AI penetration-testing framework, to automate attacks on hundreds of organizations. The framework ran reconnaissance and password spraying at machine speed, then signed in through OAuth applications that already held trusted access, so no person had to type a password. Several intrusions reached privileged cloud access in under three hours.

What is password spraying, and how fast was this campaign?

Password spraying tries a small set of common passwords against many accounts at once, staying under per-account lockout limits. ARTEX removed the human speed limit: Microsoft measured a peak of 2,900 sign-in attempts per minute, more than 2,250 customer tenants reached, and over October 10 to 14 more than 151,000 failed authentication attempts against 327 accounts in 8 organizations, about 462 attempts per account.

What is OAuth application abuse, and why does it bypass sign-in security?

Many cloud apps hold their own identities, called service principals, with permissions granted once and then trusted. Storm-0940 authenticated as those applications, so the sign-in never involved a user, a password, or a one-time code. Once inside as a trusted application, the group ran reconnaissance, changed application credentials, and tried to modify privileges to widen and keep access. Microsoft's guidance is to investigate service principal sign-ins and unexpected application credential or privilege changes.

What is the ConnectWise connection to this campaign?

Microsoft attributes the ConnectWise Cloud Backup supply-chain compromise to Storm-0940. ConnectWise Cloud Backup is a multitenant application, so one compromised application identity sits above many customer tenants at once. The campaign shows why a vendor application with broad tenant access is a beachhead: control the application identity and you inherit its reach.
