Amazon's Bee Wearables Listen to Your Conversations — Cybersecurity Check

Bee Frontier (~$75) and Bee Eclipse ($100) record and transcribe what you say — then turn it into recaps, to-do lists, and reminders. Powerful. But before one sits on your wrist, here are the five privacy questions Amazon hasn't answered.

Trigger: Entrepreneur.com, "Here's What Amazon's Groundbreaking New AI Devices Can Do," by Sherin Shibu, October 16, 2026. Product facts below are attributed to that reporting.

What are these devices?

Amazon announced plans to acquire San Francisco startup Bee in July 2025 (terms undisclosed). The new Frontier and Eclipse are the first refresh of the Bee lineup since the acquisition.

Bee Frontier
~$75
  • Wrist-worn or clip-on wearable
  • Water-resistant
  • Noise-cancelling dual microphones
  • Up to 7 days battery
  • 3 colors: lavender, sage, onyx
Bee Eclipse
$100
  • Credit-card-sized device
  • Magnetically attaches to back of smartphone
  • Same transcription software and app
  • 21+ days battery
  • 4 colors: silver, midnight, honey, blush

Both run the same job: record conversations → transcribe → produce recaps, summaries, to-do lists, and reminders in a companion app. Announced rivals: Pocket ($129) and Plaud (from $159).

What Amazon actually says about security

Amazon's claim (not independently verified): the devices "prioritize security, encrypting transcripts and only making them accessible to users." That's the full extent of the public statement. It does not specify: encryption in transit vs. at rest vs. end-to-end, who holds the keys, whether raw audio is stored separately from transcripts, or whether transcripts ever leave the user's account for processing.

The claim sounds reassuring. But "encrypted" and "accessible to users" are not the same as "only you can read it." A service can encrypt your data at rest, show it only in your account, and still process it on its own servers — where the service holds the keys.

The 5 privacy questions nobody has answered

1
Always-on or triggered?
Is the mic live continuously, or does recording start on a tap, a wake word, or a schedule? The announcement describes transcription features — not the recording trigger. This is the single most important privacy detail, and it's undisclosed.
2
What about bystanders' consent?
A conversation wearable records everyone in earshot, not just you. Nothing announced addresses consent for the people around you — and recording-consent laws vary wildly by jurisdiction (some require every party's agreement).
3
What does the encryption actually cover?
Encryption in transit? At rest? End-to-end with user-held keys? Can Amazon's own systems read the transcripts for processing, support, or abuse review? "Accessible to users" answers none of this.
4
Retention and deletion?
How long are audio and transcripts kept? Can you delete them — and does deletion mean truly gone, or just hidden from your view? No retention policy has been disclosed.
5
Do your conversations train AI models?
Audio and transcripts are extremely valuable training data. The announcement is silent on whether they feed model training — check the companion app's privacy policy and opt-outs before trusting it.

We've heard this song before

In 2018, reports surfaced that Amazon's Alexa was "always listening" — recordings that users assumed were fleeting turned out to be stored, reviewed by human contractors, and retained. Amazon insisted the assistant only recorded after a wake word; the controversy was about what happened after capture: storage, human review, and retention.

Practical steps: before you buy, and after

  • Ask for the trigger: demand a written answer on whether recording is continuous or triggered — and get it in writing, not a support chat.
  • Read the retention policy: how long is audio kept? Transcripts? Is deletion real deletion?
  • Check the app's permissions: contacts, location, background microphone, and background app refresh — strip anything the transcription job doesn't need.
  • Learn the mute gesture: find the physical mute/pause before the first meeting, not after.
  • Consent etiquette: tell people you're wearing a recorder. In some jurisdictions it's the law, not just courtesy.
  • Separate work and personal: if your employer issues one, assume work conversations are visible to your employer.

Performance claims in the announcement (e.g., filtering noisy-restaurant chatter) are product claims, not independent findings.

Cybersecurity FAQ

Is the Amazon Bee wearable always recording?

Amazon has not publicly specified whether Bee devices record continuously or only when triggered. This is one of the five open privacy questions — the announced details cover transcription features, not the recording trigger mechanism.

Does the Amazon Bee record people around you without their consent?

Any conversation-recording wearable captures bystanders as well as the wearer. The announcement does not address bystander consent, and consent laws vary by jurisdiction — some require all parties to agree to being recorded.

Are Amazon Bee transcripts end-to-end encrypted?

Amazon says the devices "prioritize security, encrypting transcripts and only making them accessible to users." The scope is unspecified — encryption in transit and at rest does not necessarily mean end-to-end encryption where only the user holds the keys.

Do Bee devices train AI models on your conversations?

The announcement does not say. Retention, deletion, and model-training policies for audio and transcripts have not been disclosed — check the companion app's privacy policy before buying.

How much do the Amazon Bee devices cost?

Bee Frontier costs about $75 and Bee Eclipse costs $100. The Frontier is wrist-worn or clip-on with up to 7 days of battery; the Eclipse is credit-card-sized, attaches magnetically to a phone, and claims 21+ days of battery.
