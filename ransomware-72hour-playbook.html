RANSOMWARE INCIDENT RESPONSE — OCT 2026

The 72-Hour Playbook: double-extortion incident response

The first 72 hours after a ransomware attack decide the trajectory of the entire incident. Hour by hour: what to contain, what to preserve, who to call, and which decisions can't wait. Print it, download it, rehearse it before you need it.

Read this first: this page is general guidance, not legal advice. Ransomware response involves privilege, sanctions, and regulatory deadlines — engage breach counsel immediately and route decisions through them. Nothing here creates an attorney-client relationship.

⚠ Reading this during a live incident? Stop reading.

  • Activate your incident response plan and call your IR retainer now — not in three hours.
  • Isolate affected hosts from the network. Do NOT power them off — memory forensics needs the live state.
  • Move to out-of-band communication — phones, a pre-arranged channel. Assume the attackers can read your email.
  • Do not contact the threat actor yet. Do not pay anything yet. Bring the CEO and General Counsel in within 60 minutes. Then come back here.
HOUR 0–4 — CONTAIN

Stop the bleeding

  • Confirm the encryption is real — sample one or two files, read the ransom note. Don't act on a hoax.
  • Isolate affected systems at the switch or by pulling network cables — do not shut them down (memory and running processes are evidence).
  • Disable VPN, remote access, and vendor tunnels — cut the paths the gang used to get in.
  • Disable compromised accounts; force password resets on admin and service accounts.
  • Move all coordination to phones or a pre-arranged out-of-band channel.
  • Open a single timestamped incident log: every action, who took it, when. One owner.
  • Photograph/screenshot the ransom note; note file extensions and the exact variant name.
  • Call your IR retainer provider. If you don't have one, engage incident response counsel — they bring the forensics firm under privilege.
  • Do NOT attempt to remove the ransomware or decrypt files yet. Do NOT contact the actor yet.

Goal by hour 4: spread stopped, evidence frozen, the right people in the (out-of-band) room.

HOUR 4–12 — ASSESS + PRESERVE EVIDENCE

Know what they took, not just what they broke

  • Engage a qualified forensic examiner before anyone touches affected systems further.
  • Preserve system state: memory images and logs, before any reimaging.
  • Identify the ransomware variant from the note and file extensions — it drives playbook decisions and decryption-tool checks.
  • Determine the likely entry vector: phishing, exposed RDP/VPN, unpatched software.
  • Answer the double-extortion question: was data exfiltrated before encryption? Check for large outbound transfers; inventory which data types lived on affected systems.
  • Check backups: are they intact, offline/immutable, and actually restorable? (Verify — don't assume.)
  • Scope the blast radius: which systems, which departments, which customer data.
  • Brief executives with facts only — no speculation, in writing or otherwise.

Goal by hour 12: variant identified, exfiltration question answered or actively investigated, evidence preserved.

HOUR 12–24 — DECIDE

Counsel, law enforcement, insurer

  • Engage breach counsel so the forensic investigation runs under legal privilege.
  • Notify your cyber insurance carrier — follow their panel-vendor requirements before spending.
  • With counsel, decide on law-enforcement engagement (e.g. FBI/IC3 or your local field office; CISA resources) — do this through counsel, not ad hoc.
  • With counsel, inventory every notification obligation: regulators, customers, partners, sector rules — and put each deadline on the log.
  • Set the ransom posture in advance: the default working position is not to pay; any exception runs through counsel and the insurer. Check free decryption catalogs for the variant.
  • Lock down external communication: one approved spokesperson, pre-approved holding statements. Staff briefed on what not to say.
  • Keep the incident log current — it is your compliance record later.

Goal by hour 24: counsel engaged, insurer notified, every notification clock mapped, posture set.

DAY 2 — NEGOTIATION POSTURE + LEAK-SITE MONITORING

Manage the second half of the squeeze

  • Finalize the ransom decision with counsel and the insurer: can you recover from verified clean backups? Does the variant have a free decryptor? Does payment buy anything real, given the data is already stolen?
  • If payment is even discussed: sanctions/OFAC-style screening with counsel before any transaction — paying a sanctioned entity creates a second crisis.
  • All threat-actor communication goes through counsel or the retained negotiator — never direct, never emotional, never off-log.
  • Begin leak-site monitoring: would you know within days if your data appeared on the gang's leak site? Set up monitoring through counsel or a service — don't browse it yourself.
  • Draft (don't send) breach notices and the customer/partner holding statement with counsel.
  • Re-verify backup integrity on an isolated network before any restore planning.

Goal by day 2: a deliberate, counseled ransom decision; leak visibility established.

DAY 3 — RECOVERY + DISCLOSURE

Restore clean, disclose right

  • Identify a clean, pre-infection recovery point — confirmed, not assumed.
  • Patch the entry vector before restoring anything, or you will be re-encrypted.
  • Restore in phases: critical systems first, into a clean recovery network.
  • Hunt for persistence and reset all credentials after the rebuild — assume the gang kept a way back in until proven otherwise.
  • With counsel, execute the notification plan: regulators, affected individuals, partners — on their deadlines, with approved language.
  • Plan for extended downtime and set realistic expectations with customers and staff.
  • Document everything: the full log becomes the basis for insurance claims, regulatory filings, and the post-incident review.
  • Schedule the post-incident review and the tabletop exercise you wish you'd run last quarter.

Goal by day 3: clean recovery underway, disclosures on track, nothing restored into a still-compromised environment.

Frequently asked questions

What should I do first when ransomware hits?

Isolate affected systems from the network immediately — disconnect them, but do NOT power them off, because memory forensics needs the live state. Move coordination to phones or another out-of-band channel (the attackers may be reading your email), disable remote access, start a timestamped log, and call your incident-response retainer and legal counsel. Do not delete anything and do not contact the attacker yet.

Should I pay the ransom?

There is no universal answer, and this page does not recommend paying or not paying. The decision belongs with your legal counsel and cyber insurer: consider whether data was exfiltrated (payment buys only a promise), sanctions screening before any payment, whether free decryption tools exist for the variant, and whether verified clean backups make payment unnecessary. Document the decision process.

Do I have to notify anyone within 72 hours?

Notification obligations depend on your jurisdiction, sector, and the data involved — engage breach counsel early to map them. As one example, GDPR-style regimes generally require notifying the supervisory authority within 72 hours of becoming aware of a qualifying breach where it applies. Your counsel should inventory every deadline: regulators, customers, partners, and insurers all have their own clocks.

How do I know if it is double extortion and not just encryption?

The ransom note or the gang's leak-site listing usually says so, but assume data theft until forensics says otherwise: check logs for large outbound transfers before the encryption timestamp, inventory which data types the affected systems held, and monitor the gang's leak site (through counsel or a monitoring service). Encryption-only recovery from backups does not address the leak half.

Why do gangs list victims on leak sites in October 2026?

The leak site is the second half of the double-extortion squeeze: public naming pressures the victim to pay even when backups make the encryption recoverable.
