How a "pack hunt" supposedly works

1. Multi-agent probing

Instead of one clever prompt, several AI instances take roles — one distracts, one drafts, one tests the classifier — so no single input looks clearly malicious.

2. Unicode / homoglyph tricks

Characters that look identical to filters but are different to the model (Cyrillic а vs Latin a) slip past pattern-based classifiers.

3. Narrative framing

Wrapping a restricted request in fiction, academic, or hypothetical scenarios — the model answers "the story," which contains the restricted content.

4. Decomposition

Splitting one harmful request into innocent-looking pieces across turns, then letting the model reassemble the intent itself.

5. Long-context smuggling

Burying the real intent deep in a huge context window, where the safety layer classifies each piece too locally to see the whole plan.

Why they can't stop it: the three structural reasons

  1. Refusal training is a veneer. Safety fine-tuning teaches the model to suppress harmful tokens — it does not delete the underlying knowledge from the weights. The capability is still in there; the refusal is a thin layer on top.
  2. The input space is infinite. Filters are trained on ordinary phrasing. Leetspeak, unusual grammar, homoglyphs, and novel framings are effectively infinite — defenders can't enumerate them all.
  3. Models can't separate commands from content. A model that reads your emails, documents, or webpages cannot reliably tell your instructions apart from instructions hidden in the text it reads. This is indirect prompt injection — see also our wild prompt-injection gallery and our AI-agent phishing check.
Defensive takeaway: If your AI assistant can read email, browse the web, or move money, keep a human in the loop before it acts. Guardrails reduce accidents; they don't make agentic AI safe to leave unsupervised.
