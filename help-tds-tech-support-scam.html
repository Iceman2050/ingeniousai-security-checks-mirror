What is the Help TDS Microsoft scam pattern?

Help TDS is a traffic-distribution network feeding fake ‘Windows Security Alert’ pages. Victims land on a page that locks the browser with exit-prevention scripts, shows a scary warning with a phone number, and the ‘technician’ who answers harvests credentials, payment details, or remote access. Microsoft never operates this way — any number on a browser alert is a scam.

What is a TDS?

A traffic distribution system: infrastructure that routes victims — often from malvertising or compromised sites — to scam pages. Help TDS specialized in tech-support scam lures.

On October 15, 2026, researchers at Infoblox reported that a long-running scam network called Help TDS — now linked to an affiliate program named Hotejka — is serving fake Microsoft Windows security alerts across more than 20,000 domains on Cloudflare's free hosting. The alerts lock your browser, show a phone number, and the call center on the other end talks victims into installing remote-access software. It is a phishing operation run through the browser: the alert is the lure, the phone call is the trap. Spot one in 30 seconds with the check below. No signup, nothing installed. How Help TDS routes one victim — stage by stage

The network is a routing business. One visitor's journey, and the signal each stage leaves for defenders:

1. EntryMalvertising or a compromised website delivers a real visitor. Signal: referrers you never bought start appearing in your analytics.
2. ProfilingThe TDS fingerprints the visitor — device, browser, geography, behaviour — and scores how convincing a target they are. The decision takes milliseconds and is invisible to the victim.
3. RoutingTop-scored visitors go to the fake Windows alert. Everyone else is sent to "verify you are human" pages. Signal: two visitors following the same link see completely different pages.
4. MonetisationThe call centre works the alert victims toward a remote-access install. The leftover traffic is farmed for notification permission and resold as spam reach. The domains are disposable; the kit and the call centre are the business.

Mechanism as described in the Infoblox disclosure of Help TDS / Hotejka, October 15, 2026 — the same reporting this page's figures come from.

Why this wave is hard to stop

  • It runs like a business: hacked websites funnel visitors into a traffic system that profiles each visitor and routes the most convincing targets to the fake alert.
  • Everyone else's traffic gets resold to fake "verify you are human" pages that ask for notification permission — then monetized with spam pushes.
  • The domains are disposable (20,000+ and rotating daily). The scam kit behind them is not — which is why defenders now fingerprint the kit, not the domains.

FAQ

Should I ever call the phone number shown on a Windows security alert in my browser?

No. Microsoft never displays a phone number in a browser security warning and never asks you to call support from a popup. Any number on a browser alert reaches a scam call center whose job is to talk you into installing remote-access software.

I clicked Allow on a notification popup from a suspicious site. What should I do?

Revoke it now: open your browser Settings, go to Privacy / Site settings / Notifications, find the site and remove it. Scam networks abuse notification permission to push spam and fake warnings straight to your desktop long after you leave the page.

Why does the fake security alert keep coming back when I try to close the tab?

The page runs scripts that force fullscreen and re-trigger the alert when you try to leave — deliberate exit-prevention, not a real system warning. Force-quit the browser (Alt+F4 on Windows, force quit on Mac) and restart without restoring tabs.

Is a fake Windows security alert a type of phishing?

Yes — it is a phishing lure delivered through the browser instead of email. The fake alert creates panic, and the “support agent” on the phone harvests your credentials, payment details, or remote access. The same rule applies: never trust an unsolicited security warning that demands immediate action.
