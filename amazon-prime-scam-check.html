SHOPPING SCAM ALERT — OCT 2026

That "cancel" button in your Amazon email? It's the trap.

Amazon itself warned Prime members about phishing emails claiming a Prime price change — with a helpful "cancel subscription" button for anyone who doesn't want to pay more. Click it and you land on a pixel-perfect fake Amazon login page built to steal your credentials and drain your accounts. A second wave claims your membership is "on hold" over a failed payment, with a 24–48 hour deadline to "update now." Five questions to find out if you'd catch it.

Short answer: never act from the message — open the Amazon app or type amazon.com yourself and check the Message Center. The one habit that stops most of these.

How the scam works — wave by wave

Wave 1: the price-change email (Amazon warned about this one)

  • You get an email that looks exactly like Amazon: Prime prices are changing on your recurring payment.
  • Two buttons: agree to the new price — or cancel your subscription to avoid it.
  • The "cancel" button feels like saving money. It takes you to a fake Amazon login page. Enter your credentials and scammers own your account — and the card on file.
  • One reported victim's account was hijacked and used to send out packages; another lost card details to a shopping spree.

Wave 2: "membership on hold" (documented by security researchers)

  • Subject lines like "Your Prime Membership On Hold" — often from a display name of "Prime Assist."
  • Claims Amazon couldn't charge you (e.g. $14.99) and your Prime Video access is suspended. Update your payment method within 24–48 hours or your membership is "permanently canceled."
  • The "Resume My Membership" / "Update Now" button opens a multi-page fake site cloning Amazon and Prime Video branding — harvesting your login, personal details and complete card number in one flow.

Sources: Budget Seniors (Amazon's warning) · Hackread · Trend Micro TrendLife · MalwareTips. Claims attributed as reported.

Amazon's real rules — what it will never do

  • Never offer a refund you didn't ask for. A surprise refund, a "wrong amount" refund, or a refund that needs a login through a link is the opening move of a scam.
  • Never ask for remote access. Amazon will not ask you to download software or grant access to your computer. Remote access is how most refund scams turn a phone call into an emptied bank account.
  • Payment only inside Amazon. Amazon takes payment only in its app, on its website, or in its own stores. Anything asking you to pay another way, on another site, or over the phone is not Amazon.
  • Verify inside the app. Open the Amazon app or type amazon.com yourself — check the Message Center and Your Orders. If it's not there, the message wasn't from Amazon.
  • Report it. Forward suspicious messages to reportascam@amazon.com. A padlock/HTTPS icon proves nothing — it only encrypts the connection to whatever site you reached.
