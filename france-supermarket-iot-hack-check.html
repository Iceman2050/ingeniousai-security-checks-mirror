FRANCE IOT ATTACK — OCT 2026

Hackers turned French supermarket fridges into ovens. How hardened is your connected equipment?

Overnight into Monday, October 12, 2026, attackers seized remote control of refrigeration systems at ~700 supermarkets across France and drove fridge temperatures to 50°C — for hours. Food worth millions of euros was dumped. Fires broke out. At one Intermarché the cold-storage room hit 50°C and triggered the automatic sprinklers; another store reported its equipment melted. And here's the part that should worry every operator of connected equipment: no one has claimed responsibility, and no ransom was demanded. The EU's agriculture commissioner wrote that “the weaponization of food has become a reality.” This 60-second check tests whether your connected physical equipment could survive the same kind of attack. What happened (verified):
• When: overnight into Monday, October 12, 2026; France's CERT-FR issued an alert October 12, updated October 13.
• Scale: Economy Minister Roland Lescure told parliament ~700 of France's ~10,000 supermarket sites were affected — Intermarché/Netto (~300), Coopérative U (~100), Carrefour (~80), Monoprix (~30), plus E.Leclerc stores.
• Method: attackers remotely manipulated the defrost function, pushed temperatures to 50°C for hours, and stretched the defrost cycle from one hour to eight — delaying alarms until staff arrived in the morning (BFM TV / AFP).
• The weak link: mostly end-of-life controllers — Danfoss AK-SM 800 (end of life 2021) and AK-SC355 (end of life 2016) — plus Dixell/Copeland XWEB systems. Danfoss had issued security alerts in March and August 2025; researchers had disclosed the vulnerabilities months earlier.
• Response: ANSSI opened an investigation, the Paris prosecutor opened a criminal case, affected stores disconnected refrigeration from networks, and CERT-FR warned attack campaigns were probably still ongoing.
• The mystery: no claim of responsibility, no public ransom demand — unusual for both ransomware gangs and hacktivists.

Why OT is different from IT

  • Attacks become physical. A breached laptop leaks data. A breached refrigeration controller spoils food, melts equipment, and starts fires. The blast radius is measured in euros and safety, not records.
  • Legacy lives forever. The controllers hit in France were end-of-lifed in 2016 and 2021 — yet still running, still networked, years after the vendor stopped patching. Replacing physical equipment costs money and downtime, so it lingers.
  • IT security tools are blind. Your endpoint agent doesn't watch a fridge controller. Anomaly detection has to happen on physical readings — temperature, pressure — which IT dashboards never see.
  • Alarms can be part of the attack. The French hackers extended the defrost cycle precisely so alarms sounded late. Any alerting wired through the compromised system is an alerting system you don't have.
  • The motive may not be money. No ransom, no claim of responsibility. When disruption itself is the objective — as the EU commissioner warned, the weaponization of essentials — the usual “we're too small to be targeted” logic collapses.
  • Exposure is global. Security scans found many such controllers directly on the public internet, with France holding a significant concentration — but the same equipment runs in stores, warehouses, and food plants everywhere.
