Cybersecurity News Today: DeadTag — Expired Domains Turned Trusted Websites Into an iPhone Spyware Network

The most alarming story in cybersecurity news today is not a hacked app or a phishing email. It is the websites you already trust. Researchers at Report URI and iVerify disclosed on October 22, 2026 that attackers bought roughly 630 expired domains that nearly 8,000 legitimate websites still load code from — forgotten chatbot widgets and analytics tags their owners never removed. Whoever owns the domain now decides what that code does, and on 248 sites it serves iPhone spyware. No click is needed: visiting the page is enough. A second campaign infected a phone through an Instagram ad that ran its exploit code the moment the ad appeared on screen. Two checks below — one for your iPhone, one for your website — take under a minute each.

Honest scope — read this first: The websites in this network are victims too — their pages were not breached in the usual sense; a third-party domain they trusted changed hands underneath them. The two research teams also count differently: Report URI's confirmed set is at least 6,600 sites; iVerify's wider count reaches 135 million visits a month across sites able to serve the spyware. Both figures are on this page, attributed, because they measure different things. The spyware targets vulnerable iOS versions — iVerify says attempts to retool the kits for current iOS are mostly non-functioning for now, so an updated phone is a hard target. And attribution is not proven: code comments lead researchers to suspect a Chinese-speaking crypto-theft operation, and the DeadTag crew itself is unattributed. Suspicion is labeled as suspicion everywhere on this page.

DeadTag: the dead tags your website forgot — someone else bought

  • The mechanism: websites load chat widgets, analytics, and other features from third-party domains. When a vendor abandons a domain and it expires, anyone can re-register it — and every site still loading that tag now runs the new owner's code. Report URI's Scott Helme named the campaign DeadTag because the tags are dead weight the sites never cut.
  • The scale (Report URI): attackers bought roughly 630 expired domains still referenced by nearly 8,000 legitimate websites. At least 6,600 sites, drawing about 49 million visits a month, load the attackers' code; 248 sites with about 8 million monthly visitors directly serve iPhone spyware, and the rest redirect visitors to spam.
  • The wider count (iVerify): hundreds of expired and re-registered domains sit on thousands of sites, including well-known brands — adding up to 135 million visits a month to sites that can serve the Coruna or DarkSword spyware when a visitor's phone qualifies.
  • The crew keeps moving: the operators register up to 10 new domains a week, and telling thousands of site owners one by one is too slow — the researchers are seeking coordination through national cybersecurity bodies. The DeadTag crew itself is unattributed and reads like a commercial traffic-delivery operation that will sell access to anyone.

Nobody hacked those sites one by one. The trust was the exploit — a script tag a developer pasted years ago kept its authority long after the company behind it walked away.

The second campaign: an Instagram ad that infected a phone by being seen

iVerify's half of the disclosure needs no website at all. An employee of a financial institution had their iPhone infected after scrolling past an Instagram advertisement about professional soccer. The malicious code started running when the ad rendered on screen — before any tap. It checked the phone's iOS version, deployed a matching exploit, stole the employee's login credentials, and searched the phone's cryptocurrency wallets. Both campaigns share the same shape: the malicious code executes inside content the victim was going to encounter anyway, then picks only the phones it can actually break into.

The payloads in both campaigns are the Coruna and DarkSword exploit-and-spyware kits, aimed at credentials and cryptocurrency — iVerify found them hunting wallets including Bitpie, TronLink, OKX and Bitget, all popular with Chinese-speaking users. The kits' code carries comments in simplified Chinese, including in the loader logic that stops the attack running twice on the same phone, and the exploit pages report back with affiliate "channel codes" that credit whichever group sent each victim. That is why researchers describe the operation as suspected — their word, and this page keeps it — financially motivated and Chinese-speaking. It is a reading of the evidence, not an identification.

Why this cybersecurity news today matters: your site can be the weapon and never know

Every prior spyware story in this series asked readers to protect a phone. DeadTag adds a second victim: the website owner. If your site loads a tag from a domain that has since expired and been re-registered, your pages are distributing whatever the new owner wants — spyware today, spam redirects tomorrow — under your brand, to your visitors, with your reputation absorbing the blame. The fix is unglamorous and entirely in your control: inventory every external domain your site loads, and delete the ones nobody can explain. A domain inventory is also the only way to find out whether you are already in the network, because the researchers notifying thousands of owners one at a time will not reach everyone quickly.

Check 1 — iPhone owners: how exposed is your phone?

Answer three questions. Everything runs in your browser; nothing is sent anywhere. This is a risk read based on the researchers' findings — not a scan of your device, and no web page can scan your iPhone.

Check 2 — website owners: score your third-party tag inventory

Paste the external domains your site loads scripts or tags from — one per line (chat widgets, analytics, tag managers, embeds, consent tools). Then answer three questions about how well you know that list.

Honest scope: this tool scores inventory hygiene only. It cannot check live domain registration from your browser — verifying who owns a domain today takes a registrar or WHOIS lookup, and that lookup is the step that actually catches a DeadTag domain. A clean score here means your list is tidy, not that every domain on it is safe.

Run the DeadTag check now

  • iPhone owners — update first: install the latest iOS (Settings → General → Software Update) and turn on automatic updates. Per iVerify, attempts to retool Coruna and DarkSword for current iOS versions are mostly non-functioning for now — the update is the defense, and it is free.
  • High-risk iPhone owners — add Lockdown Mode: if you hold significant crypto, work in finance, or have any reason to be targeted, enable Lockdown Mode (Settings → Privacy & Security). It shrinks the attack surface these kits work against. Remember that click discipline does not protect you here — neither campaign needed one.
  • Website owners — inventory every external domain: list every domain your pages load scripts, tags, or embeds from — page source, tag manager containers, and CMS plugin settings are the three places they hide. Every entry needs a named purpose and a named owner.
  • Delete dead tags: a chat widget from a vendor you dropped, an analytics tag from a finished campaign, an embed nobody remembers adding — if nobody can explain it, remove it. A dead tag is an open door with your name on it.
  • Verify ownership, not just existence: for each domain that stays, run a registrar or WHOIS lookup and confirm it is still registered to the vendor you think it is. A domain that changed hands is the DeadTag pattern exactly.
  • Put renewals on watch: the domains in this network became weapons at the moment they expired. The same applies to domains you own — an expired brand domain is a phishing kit waiting for a buyer.

Frequently asked questions

What is DeadTag, in plain terms?

DeadTag is the name Report URI's researchers gave to a criminal delivery network built on expired internet domains. Attackers bought roughly 630 expired domains that thousands of legitimate websites still load code from, through forgotten chatbot and analytics tags. When an iPhone visitor reaches one of those sites, the code can check the phone and serve spyware — no click needed.

How does a legitimate website end up serving iPhone spyware?

The site itself was never hacked in the usual sense. Years ago its developers added a third-party script — a chat widget, an analytics tag — loaded from an outside domain. The vendor let that domain expire, an attacker re-registered it, and the site kept loading code from it. Whoever controls the domain now controls what that script does in every visitor's browser.

What do Coruna and DarkSword do once they reach an iPhone?

Coruna and DarkSword are iPhone exploit-and-spyware kits. Once served to a vulnerable phone, they work to steal data — in these campaigns, login credentials and cryptocurrency. iVerify found the kits hunting for wallets including Bitpie, TronLink, OKX and Bitget, and the code reports back with affiliate channel codes so the operators can credit whoever sent each victim.

How do I protect my iPhone from DeadTag-delivered spyware?

Update iOS — that is the core defense. iVerify says attempts to retool these kits for current iOS versions are mostly non-functioning for now, so a fully updated phone is a hard target. If you hold crypto or are otherwise high-risk, enable Lockdown Mode, which shrinks the attack surface further. Click discipline does not help here, because no click is required.

I run a website. How do I know if it is part of the DeadTag network?

Inventory every external domain your pages load scripts or tags from, then check each one: do you still use the service, do you know who owns the domain today, and is it still registered to that owner? Dead widgets are the danger — a chat or analytics tag nobody on your team can identify should come off the site until it is explained.
