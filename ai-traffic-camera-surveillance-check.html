What the AI traffic cameras actually found

The West Midlands trial — the numbers

  • When/where: March 19–31, 2026 — camera trailers outside Ladywood fire station (Birmingham) and Fallings Park fire station (Wolverhampton). Results released October 2026.
  • Technology: developed and supplied by Acusensus (with AECOM). Cameras capture images through vehicle windscreens; AI analyzes them and flags suspected offences for further officer assessment.
  • Ladywood (Birmingham): ~11,144 potential seatbelt offences, ~1,163 potential mobile phone offences (some sources report 11,045 seatbelt — figures are initial AI scans, not final counts).
  • Fallings Park (Wolverhampton): ~6,081 potential seatbelt offences, ~757 potential mobile phone offences (some sources report 6,030 seatbelt).
  • Funded by: West Midlands Combined Authority with police and fire services. No fines or prosecutions — the trial measured the scale of the problem, not enforcement.
  • The disturbing images: a front-seat passenger holding a small child on the lap with no seatbelt; a driver holding a mobile phone with both hands, eyes on the screen; dozens of children without age-appropriate restraints.

How AI traffic cameras see inside your car

The data pipeline — this is the part that matters

Traditional speed cameras photograph a number plate. AI traffic cameras photograph the interior of the vehicle — driver, passengers, children, everything visible through the windscreen. The pipeline:

  1. Capture: high-resolution images of every passing vehicle, day and night.
  2. AI analysis: software flags suspected seatbelt and phone offences.
  3. Human review: flagged images go to officers for further assessment — the AI flags, humans decide.
  4. Storage: images live somewhere, for some period, accessible to someone. This is where the published record goes quiet.

The road-safety case is genuine — unrestrained children and phone-drivers are real dangers. But the privacy question is about scope, retention, and oversight, not about opposing road safety: every innocent driver and passenger photographed is collateral data. It is the same scope-creep pattern as facial recognition in shops — and the same questions apply to AI systems that watch and judge us.

5 questions to ask about any AI traffic camera program

  1. What exactly is captured? Still images only, or video? Is audio recorded? Is the number plate linked to the interior photo, creating an identified record of who was in the car?
  2. Where do the images go, and how long are they kept? Ask for the retention schedule in writing. Images of people who committed no offence should have the shortest retention and automatic deletion.
  3. Who can access the images? Named roles, logged access, no bulk exports. Ask whether images can be shared with other agencies or used for unrelated investigations.
  4. What independent oversight exists? A published Data Protection Impact Assessment (required under UK GDPR / Data Protection Act 2018), necessity and proportionality tests, and an independent reviewer — not just the deploying force grading itself.
  5. What is the trial-to-permanent pipeline? The West Midlands trial issued no fines. But a successful trial is the business case for permanent enforcement. Ask before the cameras are everywhere: under what rules would enforcement start, and who signs off?

AI traffic camera FAQ

What did the UK AI traffic camera trial find?

In a two-week trial in March 2026, AI cameras outside Ladywood fire station in Birmingham and Fallings Park fire station in Wolverhampton flagged nearly 19,000 potential offences — about 17,225 potential seatbelt offences and 1,920 potential mobile phone offences. These are potential offences flagged by the AI for officer review, not confirmed violations, and nobody was fined or prosecuted from the trial.

How do AI traffic cameras see inside your car?

The cameras photograph through the windscreen of every passing vehicle and AI software analyzes the images to flag suspected seatbelt and mobile phone offences. The flagged images are passed to officers for further assessment — the AI flags, humans decide. The system was developed and supplied by Acusensus (with AECOM) for the West Midlands Combined Authority trial.

Can AI traffic cameras be used to fine drivers?

The March 2026 West Midlands trial issued no fines — it was designed to measure the scale of the problem, not to enforce. But police forces can adopt the cameras for real enforcement: that trial-to-permanent pipeline is exactly why the data questions (what is captured, where images go, how long they are kept, who can access them, and what independent oversight exists) matter now, before the cameras are everywhere.

What are the privacy concerns with AI traffic cameras?

The cameras photograph the interior of every passing vehicle — including children and passengers who did nothing wrong. The privacy questions are about the data pipeline: where images are stored, how long they are kept, who can access them, whether non-offending images are deleted, and what independent oversight prevents mission creep from seatbelt enforcement to general surveillance.

Who decides how AI camera images are used?

In the UK, police forces and road safety partnerships decide deployments, and any enforcement use must comply with data protection law (UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018), including necessity and proportionality tests. Ask for the published data protection impact assessment of any AI camera program — it should state retention periods, access controls, and deletion rules for images of people who committed no offence.

Do AI traffic cameras reduce road deaths?

Supporters argue the cameras catch genuinely dangerous behavior — the West Midlands trial images included unrestrained small children and drivers holding phones with both hands. Whether cameras reduce casualties long-term depends on enforcement consistency and driver behavior change; the trial itself measured detection scale, not safety outcomes.
