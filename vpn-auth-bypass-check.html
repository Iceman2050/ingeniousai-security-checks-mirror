If you're exposed, do this now

“Malicious activity can resemble legitimate remote work... Patching alone may not remove access established before remediation.” — ReliaQuest, reported by The Hacker News, Oct 2026
  • Revoke every active session and token — patching closes the door, but anyone who walked in before the patch still has a seat. Terminate all GlobalProtect/Prisma Access sessions and force re-authentication.
  • Review your VPN logs — hunt for logins from unknown IPs, odd hours, and rogue hostnames like ‘kali’. Attacker sessions can look like legitimate remote work.
  • Rotate credentials — every password, API key, and certificate that touched the gateway. Assume pre-patch exposure until proven otherwise.
  • Disable auth override cookies — close session-persistence bypass paths so a patched gateway can't be re-entered on a stale cookie.
  • Patch to the fixed PAN-OS release — the May 2026 advisory's remediated version is the floor, not the ceiling. Verify the running version after upgrade.

FAQ

What is CVE-2026-0257?

CVE-2026-0257 is an authentication-bypass flaw in PAN-OS GlobalProtect and Prisma Access, disclosed in May 2026. It lets an attacker skip the login step on the VPN gateway itself — the front door to your internal network. Per Unit42 + ReliaQuest research, reported by The Hacker News, Oct 2026.

Are ransomware groups really exploiting CVE-2026-0257?

Yes. As of October 2026, the ransomware groups Qilin and Settra are actively exploiting CVE-2026-0257 to break into corporate networks through unpatched VPN gateways — per Unit42 + ReliaQuest research, reported by The Hacker News, Oct 2026.

Why isn't patching enough to stop the attacks?

Because attackers who got in before you patched may still hold valid sessions. As ReliaQuest put it: “malicious activity can resemble legitimate remote work... Patching alone may not remove access established before remediation.” Terminate active sessions, revoke tokens, and rotate credentials after patching.

What does 'kali' have to do with my VPN logs?

Attackers routinely connect from machines with default or unchanged hostnames — 'kali' is the classic one. Searching your GlobalProtect/Prisma Access logs for unknown hostnames like 'kali' is a quick, free hunt for sessions that were never legitimate.
