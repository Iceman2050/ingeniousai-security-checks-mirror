Why this cybersecurity news today story matters: the rate changed, not the bugs

Put as a plain number, Reuters reported that the discovery of software flaws rose by 100 percent in a single year, citing the VulnCheck research due out in the coming days. Finding a bug used to take a baseline level of skill, and building a digital lockpick from it, an exploit, could take an experienced professional weeks or months. That gap gave defenders breathing room: a flaw could be fixed and the update rolled out gently before anyone weaponized it. Reuters reports that AI has thrown those assumptions out. Models can subject a codebase to round-the-clock scrutiny, find one bug after another, and turn around exploits in hours.

Shane Fry, chief technology officer of Virginia-based RunSafe Security, told Reuters the demonstrated performance of AI models over the past few months had settled any doubt about whether they would change the industry. What is fundamentally changing, he said, is the timescale: not only how fast vulnerabilities are found, but how quickly adversaries can weaponize them.

Prince put the near future plainly in his Thursday interview with Reuters: "I think we're going to have a couple years where every single week it's going to feel like the sky is falling." He said AI is surfacing long-hidden weaknesses in the fabric of the internet, and that he expects busy months ahead as more of them come out.

The counterweight: more found bugs does not automatically mean more breaches

Reuters is clear that the experts agree on shorter timelines and split on what follows. Daniel Stenberg, the lead developer of the curl file transfer tool, argued the vulnerabilities AI models find tend to be exotic bugs that are difficult for rogue actors to take advantage of. Ramy Rahman, a senior principal solutions engineer at ArmorCode, said more disclosed vulnerabilities do not automatically mean more successful attacks.

The PHP flaw gave early evidence for the cautious side. Threat intelligence firm Hunt.io said it had, like Amazon and Cloudflare, seen aggressive attempts to exploit the bug. But its head of research, Esteban Borges, said that as of 2 PM Eastern Time on Thursday, October 22, the cybercriminals his firm tracks appeared to have failed to build a working tool for it. Prince, for his part, told Reuters he had seen anecdotal evidence of compromises linked to the PHP vulnerability. Both reports stand; neither is proof on its own.

Prince also gave the optimistic reading. The deluge will rattle the industry, he said, but it should be a net positive in the end: the flaws get cleaned up instead of lurking quietly where anyone who finds them first can use them in private. That is the real choice the doubling creates. Hidden bugs do not stay hidden anymore. They surface in public, on a clock defenders can plan around, or they surface in an attack.

Run the fire-drill check now

  • Build the inventory today: every internet-facing system, the exact version it runs, and a named owner for each. During the PHP scramble, teams without this list spent the emergency finding out what they ran. That is the most expensive possible use of the first day.
  • Set the emergency patch path: who approves, who ships, and how you verify the running version after a restart. Same day is the target for critical flaws in internet-facing software, because the exploit side of the clock now runs in hours.
  • Agree the shield decision in advance: a WAF rule, an access restriction, or taking the system offline on purpose. European governments took sites offline during the PHP week as a precaution. That call is far easier made in a calm meeting than at midnight under attack traffic.
  • Remove end-of-life software: it receives no fix when the disclosure lands, so every fire drill hits it at full force. PHP 8.1 and older, still running on a large share of web servers, is this week's example.
  • Watch disclosures daily: vendor advisories, the CISA KEV catalog, and the security press. On Prince's account, the next severe flaws are already sitting in critical software that has not been audited yet. The teams that hear about them first are the ones who patch first.

Frequently asked questions

What is the AI-discovered bug surge, in plain terms?

The AI-discovered bug surge is the sharp rise in software flaws being found now that AI models can read codebases around the clock. Reuters reported on October 23, 2026 that AI helped drive a doubling in flaws discovered year on year, based on VulnCheck research. The bugs are not new. Many sat hidden in old code for years. What is new is how fast they surface, and how fast exploits follow.

Did AI create these bugs?

No. The flaws Reuters describes were written by people, in some cases many years ago. The PHP flaws behind this week's government site outages had sat in the code since 2012. AI changed the search, not the code: models can subject a codebase to constant scrutiny that human reviewers never had time for, so long-hidden mistakes are being found in batches instead of one at a time.

Is the PHP flaw already being exploited with a working tool?

The reports conflict, and this page keeps both. Hunt.io said the criminals it tracks had failed to build a working exploit tool as of 2 PM Eastern on Thursday, October 22, despite aggressive attempts that Amazon and Cloudflare also saw. Cloudflare CEO Matthew Prince told Reuters he had seen anecdotal evidence of compromises linked to the flaw. Attack traffic is confirmed. A finished tool, so far, is not.

Does a doubling in discovered flaws mean a doubling in breaches?

Not automatically, and the experts Reuters quoted are split. Curl lead developer Daniel Stenberg argued that AI tends to surface exotic bugs that are difficult to exploit. ArmorCode's Ramy Rahman said more disclosed vulnerabilities do not automatically mean more successful attacks. Prince argued the cleanup is a net positive in the end: bugs that stay hidden stay available to anyone who finds them quietly.

What should a small team do first to get ready?

Start with a list. Write down every internet-facing system you run and the exact version it runs, because the first hours of an emergency disclosure go to teams who already know their exposure. Then set a same-day patch path for critical fixes, agree in advance who can shield or take a system offline, remove end-of-life software, and have one person watch disclosures daily.
