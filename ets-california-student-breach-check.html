BREAKING — OCT 17, 2026 · VIA REUTERS

One stolen login. Millions of kids' records. Is your family protected?

The California Department of Education says a hacker copied the data of millions of students — names, birth dates, addresses, even disability status — from a secure file-transfer site run by contractor Educational Testing Service. The weapon wasn't malware. It was one compromised login credential, used quietly from May to September. Five questions on whether your family is ready for the fallout.

Short answer: your child's school data doesn't live at the school — it lives at contractors you've never heard of, protected by logins you can't see. Child identity theft is the cruelest kind: a kid has no credit file to watch, so fraud can run for years before anyone notices. The five questions below are the defenses that actually matter now.

What happened — the verified timeline

WhenWhat
May 22 – Sep 18, 2026Unauthorized party used a compromised login credential to access ETS's secure file-transfer site and copy California student files, at various times.
Sep 25, 2026ETS identified the breach and began investigating.
Oct 2, 2026California Department of Education told districts ETS had stopped "normal enrollment data processing."
Oct 16, 2026ETS began notifying students and families; the department went public. Breach had run since May.
  • Who was hit: "all students enrolled in the three previous school years" — over 5.7 million enrolled in 2025–2026 alone.
  • What was taken: names, birth dates, addresses, disability status. Social Security numbers and sensitive financial info were not accessed, per the department.
  • Release status: ETS has not found that any of the information has been made public so far.
  • ETS's role: administers California statewide assessments, including English language proficiency tests.
  • ETS's response: "enhanced monitoring, strengthened access controls, and further third-party review of our security practices."

Sources: Reuters · ET Education

Three things every parent should do this week

1. Freeze your children's credit (it's free)

Kids have no credit files — that's exactly why thieves love them. A freeze at each bureau blocks new accounts in your child's name. Check the FTC's guidance for freezing a minor's credit.

2. Treat breach-notification emails as hostile until proven otherwise

Every major breach spawns phishing waves that mimic the official notice. Go to the sender's official site yourself — never click the links in the email.

3. Ask your district who else holds your child's data

This breach came from a contractor, not the school. Districts share data with testing companies, bus-routing apps, lunch-payment processors. You can't protect data you don't know exists.
