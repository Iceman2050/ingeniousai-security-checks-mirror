18 Common Ports Hackers Love — a Cybersecurity Reference

Want to hack a network, you first learn to read the map — and in the world of hacking, that map is made of ports. Every open port tells an attacker what you're running, how old your system is, and how they might break in. This page turns that map around: search the 18 most-targeted ports, see what each one exposes, and learn what to do about it.

Trigger: James Code Lab reel, "18 Common Ports That Hackers Love (And Why)," October 18, 2026. Risk ratings reflect long-established, well-documented attack patterns — not novel claims.

What is a network port?

A network port is a numbered doorway (0–65,535) that network services use to send and receive traffic. When a port is "open," a program is listening on it — which is useful for legitimate services but also gives attackers a place to knock. Closing ports you do not use shrinks what hackers can reach.

🏠 Home user vs 🖥️ server admin

For a home user

  • Your router is your firewall. Ports you should care about are the ones your router exposes to the internet — not the ones your laptop uses on your own Wi-Fi.
  • Log into your router's admin page and check: port forwarding rules, DMZ settings, and remote management. If remote management is on and you don't use it, turn it off.
  • Turn off UPnP unless you need it — it lets devices punch their own holes through your firewall without asking you.
  • Guest Wi-Fi for visitors and smart gadgets keeps one compromised bulb away from your laptop.

For a server admin

  • Default-deny firewall: close everything, open only what the service needs, to only who needs it.
  • Databases (3306, 5432, 6379, 27017) and SMB (445) should never face the public internet — bind to localhost or a private network, require authentication.
  • RDP (3389) behind a VPN, with account lockout and MFA — a bare RDP port is one of the most-attacked services on the internet.
  • Patch the dinosaur protocols out of existence: Telnet and cleartext FTP have no business on a modern network.

📡 The cleartext lesson

Some old protocols send everything in the open — including your username and password. Anyone watching the network path (public Wi-Fi, a compromised router, an ISP) can simply read them:

  • FTP (21) — logins and files travel in cleartext. Use SFTP (SSH file transfer) or FTPS instead.
  • Telnet (23) — an entire remote terminal session in cleartext. There is no fix; use SSH (22).
  • HTTP (80) — fine for redirecting to HTTPS; never type a password into an http:// page.
  • POP3 (110) / IMAP (143) — your email password in cleartext. The encrypted versions (995 / 993) exist for a reason.

Rule of thumb: if a protocol was designed before encryption was standard, assume it leaks — and use its encrypted twin.

❓ Cybersecurity FAQ

Is an open port dangerous by itself?

No — a port is just a numbered door. The danger is what answers when someone knocks: an outdated service, a weak password, or a protocol that leaks credentials. A closed port tells an attacker nothing; an open one starts a conversation.

How do I check which ports are open on my network?

Start inside: your router's admin page lists port-forwarding rules and connected devices. From outside, free port-scan services (the "shields up" style) probe your public IP and report what answers. Never scan networks you don't own or lack written permission to test.

Why is port 445 (SMB) so dangerous?

SMB is Windows file sharing — and it was the attack surface behind the WannaCry and NotPetya-era worms, which spread machine-to-machine through SMB flaws. It belongs on trusted internal networks only, never on the public internet.

Should I just block port 3389 (RDP) entirely?

If you don't use remote desktop, yes — close it. If you do, don't expose it bare: put it behind a VPN, enforce account lockout after failed logins, require strong credentials plus MFA, and restrict source IPs. Bare RDP is one of the most brute-forced services on the internet.

Are databases really attacked through their ports?

Routinely. Exposed database ports (3306 MySQL, 5432 PostgreSQL, 6379 Redis, 27017 MongoDB) are scanned constantly; open MongoDB instances were mass-ransomed in 2017, and Redis has shipped configurations without authentication. Bind databases to localhost or a private network and require authentication.

Honest scope: this page is an educational reference, not a vulnerability scanner — it can't see your network. Risk ratings describe typical exposure when a port faces the internet; a port open only to your own LAN is a different story. Never scan networks you don't own or have written permission to test.
