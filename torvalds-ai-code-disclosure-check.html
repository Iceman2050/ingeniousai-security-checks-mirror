What happened

  • The tag: since January 2026, kernel contributors using "any sort of advanced coding tool" were asked to declare it with an "Assisted-by" tag in their commits.
  • The reversal: at the 2026 Maintainers Summit, consensus formed to deprecate it. Kernel developer Sasha Levin proposed removing the documentation in an October 9 mailing-list patch — which drew 50+ replies.
  • The case against the tag: Red Hat's Jeff Layton backed removal — not everyone follows the tag anyway, it amounts to free advertising for proprietary LLM companies, and it's unclear why the kernel should be collecting that information at all.

What Torvalds said (attributed, not endorsed)

  • On the disclosure rules: replying on the kernel mailing list on October 13 to Laurent Pinchart, Torvalds called them "utterly stupid" — understanding code, he argued, has very little to do with the tools used to produce it. He mocked the logic by suggesting contributors drop their compilers and editors and just write in assembler.
  • On stage in Prague (Open Source Summit Europe, October 9, with Dirk Hohndel): he quipped that "bots talking to bots" is now real — agentic review systems like Sashiko review patches on the mailing list, and some subsystem maintainers expect submissions to have passed through them first.
  • The reversal: Torvalds previously said AI was "just not very good." Now he says he likes it for things he's bad at — like building a modern interface for his guitar-pedal project in C — called vibe coding a wonderful way to find joy in programming, and described AI as a "gateway drug" for new programmers.

The two-sided security picture

  • AI finds real bugs: Torvalds says AI tools now surface genuine security flaws, including in drivers untouched for 20 years. On the whole, he believes AI "is improving our code base."
  • But it stresses the humans: AI is "also stressing maintainers out to the point that it's a problem" — roughly three-quarters of the Maintainers Summit talks were about making AI tooling less stressful. When bots write the patches and bots review them, the human review layer gets thinner exactly when it matters most.
  • His caution: be very careful with AI "when you're doing something real and important."
  • The other camp: not everyone agrees with Torvalds — System76 banned AI-generated content from its COSMIC desktop, and NetBSD has banned AI-generated code since 2024.

Honest takeaway — Torvalds' core principle

Strip away the personalities and the debate reduces to one principle: review the code, not the provenance. A label saying "AI wrote this" tells you nothing about whether it's correct or secure — and a human-written patch can be just as broken. What is the Linux kernel's Assisted-by tag?

Since January 2026, contributors using advanced coding tools were asked to declare it with an "Assisted-by" tag in commits. After the 2026 Maintainers Summit it is being deprecated, with Sasha Levin's October 9 patch proposing to remove the documentation.

Why did Torvalds call AI disclosure rules "utterly stupid"?

His argument: understanding code has very little to do with the tools used to write it, so mandating tool disclosure misses the point. He illustrated it by joking that contributors might as well drop compilers and write in assembler.

What does "bots talking to bots" mean for review quality?

Agentic systems like Sashiko now review kernel patches on the mailing list, and some maintainers expect patches to have passed through them — so AI-written code is increasingly pre-reviewed by AI, thinning the human layer.

Does AI actually improve the Linux kernel?

Torvalds says yes on balance — AI finds genuine flaws, even in 20-year-old drivers. But it also stresses maintainers severely, and he urges caution for anything "real and important."

Which projects ban AI-generated code?

System76 banned AI-generated content from its COSMIC desktop, and NetBSD has banned AI-generated code since 2024 — the industry is split between bans, disclosure, and Torvalds' review-the-code position.
