SIGNALS CORPS RESEARCH — OCT 2026 — AI AGENT SECURITY

8,000 websites now talk to AI agents through HTTP headers — 30 tried to hack them

Researchers at Signals Corps examined ~217 million HTTP page responses and found nearly 8,000 websites using response headers to communicate with AI agents — pointers, crawler rules, machine-readable summaries. And 30 of those sites were caught attempting indirect prompt injection: instructions smuggled into header content that an AI agent might read and obey. Meanwhile only 3–10% of sites publish an llms.txt at all. Your site's headers are talking to agents right now — do you know what they're saying? Run the free audit below.

What "agent-directed headers" actually are

  • x-llms-txt — points an AI agent to a machine-readable summary of the site (6,989 sites in the study sent this one).
  • Agent pointers — 7,271 sites sent headers directing agents to look elsewhere: alternate feeds, API docs, canonical agent content.
  • Crawler rules in headers — x-robots-tag entries naming AI crawlers, telling them what they may or may not ingest. About 10% of sites use robots.txt rules to restrict AI crawlers.
  • The dark 30 — headers carrying instruction-like content: attempts to make agents ignore prior instructions, delete training data, or execute SQL-injection-style text. Many of the rest were jokes, per the researchers — but 22 were credible attack attempts.

Why headers matter: when you ask an agent to "summarize this page," your request and the page's content enter the model's context as one text stream. Anything in that stream — visible text, hidden markup, or response headers — can steer it. That's indirect prompt injection, and headers are its newest hiding place.
