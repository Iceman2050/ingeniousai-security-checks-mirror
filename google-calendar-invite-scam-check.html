Cybersecurity News Today: Fake Google Calendar Invites Land on Your Calendar Without Your Permission

The strangest story in cybersecurity news today needs no click to reach you. A calendar invite scam is phishing delivered as a meeting invitation: a scammer sends an invite, your calendar settings add it on their own, and an event you never accepted sits on your calendar with a reminder set for later. When the reminder pops, the event text does the work. It carries a link to a fake sign-in page, a file presented as a meeting document, or a phone number for a fake support line. The same lure family has two more members: spoofed Google Meet invites whose link downloads malware, and fake Google support messages that push you to reset your account. Sublime Security measured calendar-invite (ICS) phishing up about 33,000 percent between May and September 2026, as reported by TechSpot. This page states what is confirmed, then gives you two working checks: an analyzer for a meeting link or a pasted invite, and the hardening list that closes the door.

Honest scope, read this first: a fake event on your calendar does not mean your account is hacked. The Guardian's guidance on this scam is direct: the sender has no access to your account, and the compromise starts only when you click the link, download the file, or make the call. The analyzer below reads text and links only, in your browser, and flags known signals. No analyzer can prove an invite is safe, and a clean result is not a guarantee. The control that actually stops this scam is a calendar setting, and it is in the second check.

The three lures in this wave

  • Fake Calendar invites that add themselves: the invite arrives as email, and a calendar set to add invitations automatically puts the event on the calendar even when the email is filtered. The event survives after the email is gone, so the reminder pops up later for a meeting you never booked. The payload sits in the event text or its description: a sign-in link, an attachment, or a callback number.
  • Spoofed Google Meet invites: the invite looks like a Meet invitation, but the join link goes to a lookalike host or a download. Clicking it delivers malware instead of a meeting. Genuine Meet links live on one host only: meet.google.com.
  • Fake Google support messages: a prompt claims a problem with your account and pushes a reset. It arrives as an invite, an event note, or a follow-up message, and it steers you to a page or a phone number the scammer controls. Google does not run support through calendar invites, and a reset prompt you did not start is a lure.

Why does this cybersecurity news today matter?

Because the calendar and the inbox apply different rules. Gmail can send the invitation email to spam while the calendar still adds the event, so deleting or filtering the email does not remove the appointment. Sublime Security has also seen invites sent through real services, including Microsoft Teams, Zoho Calendar, and Calendly, which gives the lure a genuine sending path instead of a spam-shaped one. The titles do the rest: invites pose as invoice or payment notices and as fake voicemail alerts, subjects a busy person acts on without thinking. Check Point documented a calendar-invite campaign whose goal was the victim's Microsoft login: the invite carried the victim to a fake sign-in page, and one login opened the mailbox behind it. That is the shape to assume for any invite you did not expect: it wants a credential, a download, or a phone call, and it has all day to wait for the reminder to fire.

Four defenses decide whether this scam can reach you at all. Tick each one you have actually done. The readout states plainly whether your calendar is still open to auto-added invites, and it ends with the most important action you have not taken.

What to do now, in order

  • Change the invitation setting first. In Google Calendar on a computer: Settings, then Settings again, then Event settings under General. Set "Add invitations to my calendar" to "Only if the sender is known". Google counts a sender as known if the sender is in your contacts, in your organization, or someone you have interacted with. If you want the stricter door, choose "When I respond to the invitation in email", so an invite reaches your calendar only after you answer the email yourself.
  • Clean up what is already there. The setting applies to new invitations only. Go through the events already on your calendar, open any you do not recognize, and report it as spam from inside Calendar. Do not click Decline and do not RSVP in any direction: any response confirms to the sender that your address is live.
  • Empty the side door in Contacts. Google Contacts has a list called "Other contacts": people your account saved on its own because you interacted with them once. A scammer in that list can count as a known sender and slip past the setting. Open it and remove every sender you do not recognize.
  • Verify outside the invite, every time. If an event claims a meeting with your bank, your payroll provider, or your IT desk, confirm it through a phone number or a website you already know. The number inside the invite is the one thing you never call.
  • If you already clicked or called: a fake event alone does not compromise your account, but acting on it can. If you signed in on a page the invite sent you to, change that password now at the real site, turn on two-factor authentication if it is off, and review the account's recent activity. If you downloaded a file, treat the device as exposed and get it checked before you sign in to anything sensitive from it.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Google Calendar invite scam, in plain terms?

It is phishing delivered as a meeting invitation. A scammer sends a calendar invite that your settings add to your calendar automatically, so an event you never accepted appears with a reminder set. The event text carries the payload: a link to a fake sign-in page, a file presented as a meeting document, or a phone number for a fake support line. The same lure family includes spoofed Google Meet invites whose link downloads malware, and messages that pose as Google support and push you to reset your account. Sublime Security measured calendar-invite phishing up about 33,000 percent between May and September 2026.

Does a fake event on my calendar mean my account is hacked?

No. The Guardian's guidance on this scam is direct: a fake event on your calendar does not mean your account is compromised, and the sender has no access to your account or your other events. The compromise starts only if you act on the event: click its link and sign in, download its file, or call its number and follow the instructions. If you did none of those, report the event as spam and tighten the invitation setting. If you did click or call, treat it as a real incident: change the password at the real site, and check your account's recent activity.

How do I stop calendar invitations from strangers landing on my calendar?

In Google Calendar on a computer, open Settings, then Settings again, and find Event settings under General. Set 'Add invitations to my calendar' to 'Only if the sender is known'. Google counts a sender as known if the sender is in your contacts, in your organization, or someone you have interacted with. For stricter control, choose 'When I respond to the invitation in email', so nothing lands on the calendar until you answer the email. The setting applies to new invitations only, so review events that are already there. Also open Google Contacts, check 'Other contacts', and remove senders you do not recognize, because an auto-saved sender can count as known.

Should I click Decline on a suspicious calendar invite?

No. Any response, including Decline, tells the sender your address is live and monitored, which invites more attempts. Open the event in Google Calendar and use Report as spam instead, which removes it without answering the sender. Then verify any real meeting the event claims to be about through a phone number or a website you already know, never the number or the link inside the event itself.
