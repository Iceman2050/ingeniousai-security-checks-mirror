Cybersecurity News Today: SafePay Ransomware — 600+ Organizations, Five Known CVEs, and Backups Wiped First

The ransomware story in cybersecurity news today carries six government seals. On October 20, 2026, CISA, the FBI, the U.S. Secret Service, Germany's LKA Rheinland-Pfalz, the UK National Crime Agency and the North West Regional Organised Crime Unit published a joint advisory on SafePay, one of the most active ransomware groups of the year. The count: more than 600 organizations across critical infrastructure sectors since August 2024. The method is not exotic: stolen passwords, VPN weaknesses, phishing, and five known vulnerabilities in edge and backup products, every one of them already patched by its vendor. Then, before the encryption starts, they go for the backups. The four-question check below tells you whether your network matches the victim profile.

Honest scope — read this first: An advisory is intelligence, not a notice that you are infected. The 600+ figure is a minimum count built from investigations through August 2026, spread across North America, South America, Europe and Australia; it is not a target list. Running one of the named products does not mean SafePay has touched you; it means an unpatched one matches the door their scripts try first. And one line from the agencies deserves to be read before any incident, not during one: paying a ransom does not guarantee file recovery.

The advisory, exactly as published

  • Who published it: CISA with the FBI, the United States Secret Service, the German Landeskriminalamt Rheinland-Pfalz, the UK National Crime Agency and the North West Regional Organised Crime Unit, on October 20, 2026, drawing on investigations conducted through August 2026.
  • The group: SafePay has operated since August 2024 as a private group with no affiliates, unusual in an industry built on renting ransomware to partners. NCC Group counted it the most active ransomware group in May 2026, with 70 claimed attacks that month; BlackKite ranked it third most active across the first four months of 2026, with a focus on Germany.
  • Initial access: exposed or stolen credentials, brute-force password attacks, phishing, exploitation of VPN services (investigators observed connections originating from Mullvad VPN addresses), and automated scripts that exploit known vulnerabilities in internet-facing systems.
  • Inside the network: actors take over existing accounts or create new administrative ones, move with legitimate tools (PsExec, WinRM, RDP), enumerate shares with ShareFinder.ps1 and SharpShares.exe, disable security measures, and steal data before encrypting it.
  • The pressure: double extortion — encryption plus a dedicated leak site threatening publication — backed in some cases by phone calls to victims.

Every one of the five CVEs named in the advisory has a vendor fix available: 100 percent of them. That is the sentence to sit with. SafePay's edge is not a secret weapon; it is the gap between a fix existing and a fix being installed, multiplied by passwords that were never rotated and backups that were never isolated.

Read the product column again: firewalls, VPN gateways, and the backup server itself. The list is the attack plan in miniature: get in at the edge, then own the system that holds the recovery copies. CVE-2024-40711 deserves its own line: an unauthenticated attacker who can reach a Veeam server can run code on it, and Veeam is where the backups live.

Why this cybersecurity news today matters: the backup is part of the target

Older ransomware advice ended at "keep good backups." SafePay's playbook is why that advice now has a second half. The advisory says the group may delete volume shadow copies and reset network-attached storage devices that hold backups, before encryption. A NAS on the same network, administered with the same credentials as the servers it protects, is not a recovery plan; it is a second target with the word backup written on it. Analysts believe SafePay also sits behind the November 2024 attack on the U.S. business services firm Conduent, which agreed an $80 million class-action settlement over that breach on October 16, 2026; the attribution is reported as a belief, not a confirmed finding, and it is labeled that way here. What is confirmed is the shape: credentials and known flaws in, legitimate tools for movement, data out, backups destroyed, then encryption.

The SafePay exposure check — four questions

Answer for your whole network, not your best server. Everything runs in your browser; nothing is sent anywhere.

Run the SafePay check now

  • Patch the edge first, the five CVEs before everything: if you run Cisco ASA or Firepower, Veeam, or SonicWall products, confirm the fixes for CVE-2020-3259, CVE-2023-20269, CVE-2023-27532, CVE-2024-40711 and CVE-2024-40766 are installed. Then put every VPN, firewall and remote-access device on a patch schedule with a name attached to it.
  • Put MFA on every remote-access account: no exceptions for vendor, service or break-glass accounts; those are the accounts brute force and stolen passwords find first. Rotate credentials that have ever been shared by email or ticket.
  • Hunt for accounts you did not create: SafePay establishes control by taking over or creating accounts. Audit administrative accounts and recent privilege changes; an admin account nobody remembers creating is an incident, not a curiosity.
  • Restrict the legitimate tools: PsExec, WinRM and RDP are administration tools and SafePay's movement kit at the same time. Limit them to the hosts and accounts that need them, alert on ShareFinder.ps1 and SharpShares.exe execution, and watch for security tools being disabled.
  • Isolate the backups and prove a restore: keep at least one copy offline, immutable, or behind separate credentials, and put NAS devices on their own credentials and network segment. Then test a full restore. An untested backup is a hope, and SafePay's playbook is built on that hope.
  • Know the reporting route before you need it: the advisory carries the full IOC and TTP lists and asks victims to report to CISA or the FBI. Decide in advance who calls, who isolates, and who talks to staff, because the group adds phone pressure to the leak threat.

Frequently asked questions

What is SafePay ransomware, in plain terms?

SafePay is a ransomware group that has operated since August 2024 as a private crew with no affiliates. It breaks into business networks through stolen passwords, VPN weaknesses and known flaws in edge devices, steals data, then encrypts systems and threatens to publish the stolen files. A joint advisory from CISA, the FBI, the U.S. Secret Service and European partners, published October 20, 2026, counts more than 600 victim organizations.

Which vulnerabilities does the SafePay advisory name?

Five: CVE-2020-3259 and CVE-2023-20269 in Cisco ASA and Firepower VPN and web services; CVE-2023-27532 and CVE-2024-40711 in Veeam Backup and Replication, the second an unauthenticated remote code execution flaw rated CVSS 9.8; and CVE-2024-40766 in SonicWall SonicOS access control. Every one of the five is a known vulnerability with a vendor fix available. SafePay's scripts look for the unpatched ones.

How does SafePay get into a network?

The advisory lists exposed or stolen credentials, brute-force password attacks, phishing, and exploitation of VPN access and internet-facing systems. Investigators observed connections coming from Mullvad VPN addresses, and the group uses automated scripts to exploit known CVEs in edge devices. Once inside, actors take over or create administrative accounts, move with legitimate tools such as PsExec, WinRM and RDP, map network shares, steal data, and disable security measures before encrypting.

Why does SafePay attack backups?

Because recovery is the victim's way out, and SafePay closes it. The advisory says the group may delete volume shadow copies and reset network-attached storage devices that hold backups, so when encryption lands, the obvious restore points are already gone. Backups that sit on the same network, reachable with the same credentials as everything else, are part of the target. Isolated, immutable or offline backups with separate credentials are the defense, and only a tested restore proves they work.

What should an organization do first against SafePay?

Start at the edge, in the advisory's own order: patch VPNs, firewalls and other internet-facing devices, starting with the five named CVEs if you run Cisco, Veeam or SonicWall products. Put MFA on every remote-access account, including vendor and service accounts. Audit administrative accounts for ones you did not create. Restrict administrative tools and privileges to the people who need them. Then isolate backups from the network they protect and test a restore.
