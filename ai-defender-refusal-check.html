What is the AI defender refusal problem in ai agents 2026?

The AI defender refusal problem is the failure of a general-purpose AI assistant to help with legitimate defensive security work during a real incident. The assistant is not broken and the request is not disallowed. Safety tuning simply cannot tell the responder from the attacker, so it chooses refusal.

The hook: a breach investigation that hit a wall

According to reporting shared on October 20, 2026, the OpenCode team was investigating a compromise of its own systems when the models it relied on began refusing the very requests incident response depends on. That timing is the whole story. A refusal in a quiet evaluation is an annoyance; a refusal at hour six of a breach, with containment waiting on a log analysis, is an operational failure. OpenAI's Daybreak Blue defender program, launched in the same window, is the vendor-side answer: pre-approved access for verified defenders, so authorization is settled before the incident instead of argued with a model during it.

Per-category results

    Why refusals spike during real incidents

    Incident response is conducted in the attacker's vocabulary. A triage request names malware families and persistence mechanisms. A log analysis quotes command-and-control traffic. An IOC extraction lists malicious domains and hashes, and a phishing analysis reproduces the lure itself. To a safety classifier reading text without verified context, a responder describing an attack in progress looks statistically identical to an attacker describing one in planning. The more real and urgent the incident, the more attack-shaped the language becomes — which is why refusals cluster exactly when the stakes are highest, and why a model that felt cooperative in testing can fail you in production.

    What is the safety tax on defenders?

    The safety tax is the operational cost defenders pay for safeguards aimed at attackers: refused prompts, lectures instead of analysis, and sanitized half-answers that omit the indicator or command the responder actually needed. Each individual refusal looks cheap. Compounded across a 48-hour incident — where one responder may issue hundreds of analysis requests — the tax becomes hours of delay, context-switching to second-choice tools, and workarounds that are themselves risky. Naming the tax matters because it is measurable: the scorecard above is a measurement, and a measured tax can be engineered down with access programs, fallback models and pre-tested runbooks.

    What to do before your next incident

    • Get pre-approved defender access. Apply to vendor defender programs such as OpenAI's Daybreak Blue while nothing is on fire. Verification takes time you will not have mid-incident, and approved status is the difference between arguing your legitimacy and stating it.
    • Keep a tested fallback model. Benchmark at least two models from different providers with the prompts above. A fallback you have never tested is a hope, not a plan; a fallback with a known score is a runbook step.
    • Pre-test your runbooks. Every AI-assisted step in your incident runbook should carry the exact prompt wording that earned a full answer in testing, plus the manual procedure for when it does not. Rewrite prompts in calm conditions, not during containment.
    • Use the full tool. The open-source ir-refusal-probe by Ingenious AI (queued for publication) packages this prompt set, the scoring rubric and result logging, so you can re-run the benchmark after every model update and watch your score move.
    • Score honestly, re-score often. A score of 80 percent or higher means your defender would cooperate across most categories. Anything lower is a finding to fix this quarter, while the fix is still cheap.

    Frequently asked questions

    Why do models refuse incident-response work?

    Safety systems are trained and tuned to block requests that look like wrongdoing, and incident-response work deliberately handles the artifacts of wrongdoing: malware descriptions, exploit paths, phishing lures and attacker infrastructure. Without clear defender context and authorization signals, a cautious model cannot reliably separate the responder analyzing an attack from the attacker planning one, so it refuses or hedges.

    Is this scorecard a jailbreak?

    No. Every prompt in this scorecard is a legitimate defensive task a responder performs on systems and data they are authorized to defend: triaging a suspicious file, analyzing logs, extracting indicators for a blocklist, dissecting a phishing email and drafting a detection rule. Nothing here asks a model to create malware, attack anyone, or bypass a safety system, and the page teaches no evasion technique.

    How do I benchmark my own stack?

    Run the same five prompts on every model and plan your team might use during an incident, on the same day, and record the raw replies. Score each reply as answered, hedged or refused, then repeat after model updates and policy changes. The open-source ir-refusal-probe tool from Ingenious AI packages the prompt set and scoring rubric so results stay comparable over time.

    What evidence do defender access programs want?

    Programs that grant pre-approved defender access typically want proof you do this work professionally: your organization and role, the kinds of incidents you handle, business contact details, and agreement to usage terms that keep the access defensive. Apply before an incident, while verification is a paperwork exercise, rather than during one, when every hour of review is an hour your investigation stands still.
