Key facts

  • Identification precedes blocking: a bot you cannot name is a bot you cannot deliberately allow, throttle or stop.
  • 5 signals combine: user-agent declaration, published IP ranges, behaviour, robots compliance and reverse-DNS or registry records.
  • The database is dated and vendor-documented: agent identities come from vendors' own published documentation, with the date checked — not from folklore lists.

How do you identify a web scraping bot?

You identify a bot by combining signals, because no single one is proof. Honest crawlers declare a recognisable user-agent and publish the IP ranges and verification methods they use; dishonest scrapers borrow those names. Matching the declared identity against the vendor's own documentation — ranges, behaviour and robots conduct — is what separates identification from guessing.

  • User-agent declaration: the name the visitor claims — necessary, never sufficient.
  • Published IP ranges: does the traffic arrive from the ranges that vendor actually publishes?
  • Reverse DNS and registry records: does the address resolve back to the claimed operator?
  • Behaviour: request rate, path order, asset loading and session shape — crawlers, agents and scrapers move differently.
  • Robots and policy conduct: does the visitor respect robots.txt and your published agent policy, as its documentation promises?

What do you do with an identification?

  • Allow the verified agents you want — search, answer engines and your customers' assistants.
  • Throttle the heavy but legitimate — rate limits keep a keen crawler from becoming an outage.
  • Block the undeclared, the spoofed and the abusive — by identity and behaviour, not by country-sized net blocks.
  • Log and revisit: new agents launch constantly; an identification list ages in weeks, so the database behind it must be maintained and dated.

Frequently asked questions

What is web scraping by AI bots?

Web scraping is automated visiting and extraction of a site's content at scale. AI bots do it to train models, power answer engines, compare prices or run agents. Some declare themselves and follow the rules; others spoof identities or ignore robots directives — which is why identification combines five signals instead of trusting one.

How can I tell which bots visit my site?

Start from your server logs: record the user-agent, then verify it against the vendor's published IP ranges and documentation, check reverse DNS, and compare behaviour and robots conduct with what that vendor promises. AgentBlock's database does this matching against dated, vendor-documented records for the known agent population.

Should I block all AI bots?

That is a business decision with real costs: blocking answer-engine and search crawlers removes you from the answers their users read, while allowing every undeclared scraper gives your content away unthrottled. Identification exists so the choice can be per-agent — allow, throttle or block — instead of one blunt rule for everything automated.
