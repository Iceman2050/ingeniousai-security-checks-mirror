AI TOY PRIVACY — EILIKO / EILIK — OCT 2026

Your child's toy may be listening. 13 million chats were left open.

In October 2026, The Verge reported that independent security researcher Sammy Azdoufal found the cloud service behind Eiliko — a pocket-sized AI robot companion made by Energize Lab and sold on Amazon — essentially unprotected. Azdoufal could reportedly read roughly 13 million conversations from what appeared to be the entire active worldwide fleet of about 68,000 robots, with more than 31,000 profiles belonging to children in the US — and he could remotely change what the toys say. When the reporter asked his own Eiliko, “do you ever tell anybody else what I'm saying?” it answered: “your secrets are safe with me.” They were not. This 60-second check is for every parent with an internet-connected toy at home. Free, no signup.

What the reporting found (The Verge, Oct 2026):
• ~13 million conversations stored as readable text on servers in China, from ~68,000 robots — seemingly the whole active fleet.
• 31,000+ profiles belonging to US children — the US is the largest market.
• Profiling, not just storage: the service built profiles with names, ages, birthdays, relationships, occupations, and assessments of personality and emotional vulnerability — kept with email addresses, IP addresses, and searchable transcripts.
• Public photos and audio: some photos — including apparent images of children from a related camera-equipped device — and short audio clips were reachable at public web addresses, contradicting the privacy policy's claim that recordings are deleted immediately.
• Remote hijack: the researcher rewrote a robot's memory and behavior from afar, making it produce false and inappropriate content — and says the entire fleet could be altered at once.
• No company response: the reporter contacted Energize Lab through 10 email addresses and LinkedIn and received no substantive answers.

Why this one matters — what makes it different

  • It wasn't a hack; it was left open. The researcher reportedly needed almost no effort to reach the data — the cloud service was essentially unprotected, not breached through sophistication.
  • Children were the product's audience. Over 31,000 of the exposed profiles reportedly belonged to children in the US — the largest market for a toy sold as a cute digital pet.
  • The toy lied about privacy. Asked whether it tells anyone what you say, it answered “your secrets are safe with me” — while full transcripts sat on unprotected servers.
  • Strangers could talk back. Remote manipulation of the robot's speech means a child could hear words chosen by an attacker — not just have their words overheard.
  • Silence from the maker. With no substantive response to the reporter, parents have no fix timeline, no deletion path, and no assurance it won't happen again.
  • The pattern repeats. The same researcher previously disclosed similar flaws in robot vacuums and baby monitors in 2026. Internet-connected devices marketed to families keep shipping with the same missing basics.
