Microsoft Hacked on X: Clippy Crypto Scam (Fake Apology)

On October 1, 2026, Microsoft got hacked — not its servers, not its cloud, but its voice. Attackers seized the official @Microsoft X account, with more than 13 million followers, and spent roughly 30 minutes turning one of the most trusted brand accounts on the internet into a billboard for a fake Clippy cryptocurrency. Then came the twist that makes this incident worth studying: an "apology" post appeared on the account disavowing the token — and Microsoft later confirmed that post was also written by the attackers. When even the incident response is attacker-controlled, trust itself becomes the attack surface.

What is the Microsoft X Clippy crypto hijack?

The Microsoft X Clippy crypto hijack is the October 1, 2026 takeover of Microsoft's official X account (@Microsoft), which unknown attackers used to promote a fraudulent $Clippy memecoin. The compromised account followed @clippymsftcto — a purpose-built account impersonating Clippy, Microsoft's retired Office assistant — reposted its message asking how many likes it would take to bring Clippy back, and replaced Microsoft's profile picture with a Clippy image. A companion account, @ClippyMSFT, promoted the $Clippy token while falsely claiming its liquidity pool was "paired directly with $MSFT," Microsoft's Nasdaq ticker. There is no legitimate connection between Microsoft, its stock, and the token.

The 30-minute timeline

~T+0 min — the takeover becomes visible. The @Microsoft account follows @clippymsftcto and reposts its "how many likes to bring Clippy back" message (one report says the post set the bar at 500,000 likes). Microsoft's avatar changes to a Clippy image.

Minutes later — the token push. The @clippymsftcto account is suspended by X, but @ClippyMSFT keeps promoting the $Clippy token, claiming the liquidity pool is paired with $MSFT and reportedly suggesting the pools are backed by actual Microsoft shares. Those backing claims are unverified.

~T+30 min — the fake apology. A post appears on @Microsoft disavowing the token: it says Microsoft is aware of a cryptocurrency being promoted in connection with $MSFT stock, including unauthorized use of the Clippy brand and Microsoft intellectual property. It is deleted shortly after.

After — the real response. Microsoft secures the account, removes the unauthorized posts, and confirms through its spokesperson that the access was unauthorized and the posts did not come from the company. The company says it will pursue legal action against the token's promoters.

Reporting: BleepingComputer (via multiple outlets), The Verge, SecurityWeek, news.bitcoin.com, October 2026.

The twist: even the apology was fake

This is the detail that elevates the incident from a routine account takeover to a case study. The apology post read like responsible incident communication — a company becoming aware of abuse of its brand and disavowing it. Microsoft later told the media that the message was posted by the hackers, not by company representatives, and it was deleted.

The lesson is uncomfortable: during an active account compromise, you cannot trust the account's own statements about the compromise. The attackers controlled the narrative channel itself. Anyone who saw the apology and thought "good, Microsoft is handling it" was reading attacker-written copy. Real verification requires a second channel — the company's website, a different verified account, or reputable press confirming the statement through a spokesperson.

How the scam worked: trust laundering

The $Clippy token had no technology story and no legitimate backing. What it had was borrowed credibility: for 30 minutes, the promotion appeared to come from Microsoft itself. That is the entire mechanism — laundering an unknown token through a trusted brand's distribution channel. The Clippy nostalgia was the hook (a beloved, retired mascot "coming back"), the @Microsoft repost was the credibility, and the fabricated $MSFT pairing was the pseudo-financial logic meant to make the token look structurally tied to a real company.

It is the same reason these scams keep targeting official accounts rather than building their own audiences: trust converts. A memecoin promoted by an anonymous account is noise; the same memecoin apparently endorsed by a 13-million-follower verified brand account is a plausible opportunity.

How did Microsoft get hacked? Still undisclosed

Microsoft has not said how the attackers got in. The company says only that it confirmed unauthorized access and is continuing to investigate. That honesty matters — and the gap is worth naming rather than filling with guesses. Security researchers consider several plausible paths, none of them confirmed:

  • Phishing a social media manager — credential theft against the human with posting access.
  • SIM swapping — hijacking the phone number linked to the account to intercept SMS-based 2FA codes. This is the technique regulators attributed to the January 2024 SEC X account compromise.
  • Password-reset email hijack — compromising the email account used for X password resets.
  • Session-token theft — infostealer malware harvesting an active login session, bypassing passwords entirely.
  • Third-party tool compromise — a social media management platform with delegated posting access getting breached instead of X itself.

Any writeup that tells you definitively which one it was is inventing facts. What is confirmed is the outcome: the attackers could follow, repost, and change the avatar — full account control, however briefly.

The pattern: this playbook keeps working

  • SEC, January 2024 — the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission's X account was SIM-swapped and used to post a fake Bitcoin ETF approval announcement, briefly moving markets.
  • @MicrosoftIndia, June 2024 — Microsoft's India account was hijacked and used for a Roaring Kitty impersonation pushing wallet-drainer malware.
  • Vlad Tenev, July 2026 — the Robinhood CEO's X account was hijacked to promote a fake meme coin — the same playbook, per reporting.
  • @Microsoft, October 2026 — the Clippy token hijack described on this page.

The through-line: high-trust accounts with weak operational security around posting access. The scam changes costumes (ETF news, Roaring Kitty, memecoins, Clippy nostalgia); the vulnerability is the same.

How to protect a brand account

As one security briefing on this incident put it, social media account hygiene must be treated as an enterprise security control, not a communications-department concern. The checklist:

  • Hardware MFA, no SMS 2FA. Security keys (FIDO2) resist phishing and SIM swapping; SMS codes resist neither.
  • Least-privilege posting access. Few people, named accounts, no shared logins — every post attributable to a person.
  • No password reuse; a password manager for the team. The reset-email account needs the same protection as the X account itself.
  • Audit third-party app access. Revoke social management tools you no longer use; each one is a side door.
  • An out-of-band incident plan. Decide in advance how you will communicate if the account itself is compromised — because as this case shows, you may not be able to use it.

If you follow brand accounts: when a trusted account posts something surprising — a crypto token, a giveaway, an urgent apology — check a second channel before believing or acting on it. The Microsoft case proved the apology itself can be fake.

Frequently asked questions

What happened in the Microsoft X Clippy crypto hijack?

On October 1, 2026, unknown attackers seized Microsoft's official X account (@Microsoft, more than 13 million followers) for roughly 30 minutes. The compromised account followed @clippymsftcto, an account impersonating the Clippy assistant, reposted its message asking how many likes it would take to bring Clippy back, and swapped Microsoft's avatar for a Clippy image. A second account, @ClippyMSFT, promoted a $Clippy token, falsely claiming its liquidity pool was paired directly with $MSFT, Microsoft's stock ticker. There is no legitimate connection between Microsoft and the token.

Was the apology post from Microsoft real?

No. About 30 minutes into the incident, an apology post appeared on the @Microsoft account disavowing the token — and Microsoft later confirmed that post was also published by the attackers, not the company. It was then deleted. Microsoft's actual position came later through its spokesperson: the company confirmed unauthorized access, said the posts did not come from Microsoft, secured the account, removed the unauthorized posts, and said it plans legal action against the token's promoters.

Is the $Clippy token connected to Microsoft or MSFT stock?

No. The token's promoters claimed its liquidity pool was paired directly with $MSFT, Microsoft's Nasdaq ticker, and some reportedly claimed the pools were backed by actual Microsoft shares. Microsoft denies any link: the company says it never authorized the creation, promotion, or use of any cryptocurrency token associated with Clippy, Microsoft, or $MSFT, and that holders get no ownership rights in Microsoft Corporation. The reported claims of backing are unverified.

How did the attackers get into Microsoft's X account?

Microsoft has not disclosed the access vector — it says only that it is continuing to investigate. Security researchers consider several plausible paths, none of them confirmed: phishing a social media manager, SIM swapping against the phone number linked to the account (the technique used in the 2024 SEC X account compromise), hijacking the email account used for password resets, session-token theft via infostealer malware, or a compromised third-party social media management tool.

How can I tell whether a brand account post is legitimate?

Verify through a second channel before trusting or acting on it. Check the brand's official website, a second verified social account, or a reputable news outlet covering the announcement. Red flags include: the account suddenly following or amplifying unknown accounts, a changed avatar, crypto or giveaway promotions from a brand that never does them, and urgency language pushing you to act fast. The Microsoft case proves even an apology post on the official account can be attacker-controlled.
