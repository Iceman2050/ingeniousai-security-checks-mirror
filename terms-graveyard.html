In memoriam: your privacy

Every clause below is real, quoted briefly, dated, and sourced. Some died by backlash. Some were quietly buried in a "policy update." Two were killed by a court order. None of them will be missed — which is exactly the problem. When a clause dies, check who benefits.

Honest banner: Every entry on this page is backed by published reporting or an official record, linked per entry. If a clause's deletion can't be cited, it doesn't get a tombstone — there are no invented entries here. Dates are publication/effective dates from the cited sources.
† DIED OCTOBER 17, 2026 — BY BACKLASH

Meta — the AI-"train" sentence

"Meta may use any content from such domains, web pages, or URLs to provide, develop, improve, research, train, and maintain the integrity of, the Meta Company Products, services, and features provided under these Commercial Terms and any other applicable supplemental terms."

When it died: October 17, 2026. Meta cut the sentence from its Commercial Terms (effective October 30, 2026) after publishers and legal scholars — including Nieman Lab and Cornell professor James Grimmelmann — warned the wording read as a blank check to scrape news sites for AI training, possibly overriding robots.txt. The Verge reported the removal the same day.

What replaced it: narrowed crawling language limited to advertising and business products — the word "train" is gone. Meta's spokesperson said the change doesn't alter how its web crawlers actually operate and that robots.txt still signals owner preferences. The clause died; the crawlers didn't.

Source: RR Tech Pulse — "Meta removes AI training from site terms" (Oct 17, 2026)

† DIED ~MAY 2026 — QUIETLY, IN A SETTINGS REDESIGN

Google Chrome — the on-device AI assurance

"Without sending your data to Google servers."

When it died: spotted ~May 2026. The on-device AI toggle in Chrome's Settings UI used to carry that exact sentence — a privacy representation users could rely on when deciding whether to leave the default toggle in place. In the current Settings, the assurance is gone, and the toggle was moved out of the System block into its own section, visually decoupling it from surrounding device controls.

What replaced it: the same toggle, with the same default, minus the sentence promising your data stays local. No announcement — just a quieter UI.

Source: That Privacy Guy — "Google quietly removes the on-device AI privacy assurance from Chrome's Settings UI"

† DIED SEPTEMBER 2024 — DEFAULT-OPTED INTO THE TRAINING SET

LinkedIn — "your posts are not training data"

"Data for Generative AI Improvement" — a new toggle, switched ON by default for all eligible members.

When it died: September 2024. LinkedIn updated its privacy policy to use member data — posts, comments, articles, profile information, work history — to train generative AI models, without explicit consent. Users discovered the opt-out buried in Settings → Data Privacy, sparking backlash covered by 404 Media and The Verge.

What replaced it: default-on AI training with a manual opt-out that is explicitly not retroactive — anything posted before you found the toggle is already embedded and stays in the model. Training on you was opt-out; it never asked.

Source: Canopy Press — "7 AI Opt-Out Settings You Should Change Today" (summarizes 404 Media / The Verge coverage)

† DIED OCTOBER 18, 2024 — EFFECTIVE NOVEMBER 15, 2024

X (Twitter) — the 18-month data retention cap

Previous policy: X would keep user information for a maximum of 18 months. New policy: "We keep your profile information and content for the duration of your account."

When it died: October 18, 2024. In the same update, X added a "Third-party collaborators" paragraph to its Privacy Policy: "If you do not opt out, in some instances the recipients of the information may use it for their own independent purposes... including, for example, to train their artificial intelligence models, whether generative or otherwise." At the time, X had not specified where users could even toggle the opt-out.

What replaced it: indefinite retention, third-party AI licensing as a potential revenue stream, and — in the same Terms update — a clause fining anyone viewing more than a million posts a day $15,000 in "liquidated damages."

Source: TechCrunch — "Elon Musk's X is changing its privacy policy to allow third parties to train AI on your posts" (Oct 17, 2024)

† DIED JUNE 2023 — BY FTC ENFORCEMENT ($75,000)

1Health.io (Vitagene) — the restrictive privacy policy you signed up under

The old policy's protections: narrower third-party sharing, narrower disclosure purposes — for your genetic and health data.

When it died: June 2023. The FTC's complaint alleged the genetic testing company retroactively and surreptitiously changed its privacy policy — broadening the third parties with whom consumer genetic information could be shared and expanding the purposes of disclosure — without notifying consumers or obtaining consent, even though those consumers had provided DNA under the previous, materially more restrictive policy.

What replaced it: a settlement: $75,000 fine, destruction of retained DNA samples, and a ban on sharing collected health data with third parties. The lesson the FTC keeps repeating: material changes need clear, conspicuous notice and cannot be applied retroactively.

Source: Nutter McClennen & Fish — FTC action on retroactive privacy policy changes (June 2023)

† DIED AUGUST 27, 2026 — BY COURT ORDER

Radaris — the entire broker

The entire business model: aggregating your name, address history, relatives, and records into a public profile — sold to anyone.

When it died: August 27, 2026. The New Jersey Superior Court entered a final default judgment against Radaris.com, Rehold.com, and Trustoria.com in Atlas Data Privacy Corporation, et al. v. Radaris.com, et al., finding violations of New Jersey's Daniel's Law (protecting home addresses and unpublished phone numbers of law enforcement, judges, and other covered persons) and awarding seizure of the radaris.com domain to Atlas Data Privacy Corporation. The site now serves a court notice.

What replaced it: a seizure notice and permanent injunctions restraining disclosure of covered persons' protected information. One of the most stubborn opt-out flows in the industry — solved by seizing the domain.

Source: radaris.com — official court-order transfer notice; see also KrebsOnSecurity, "Data Broker Radaris Loses Domains in Privacy Fight" (Sept 16, 2026)
† DIED 2026 — BY SETTLEMENT

PeekYou — the entire broker

The entire business model: a people-search engine stitching your public fragments into one searchable dossier.

When it died: 2026. Atlas Data Privacy Corporation sued PeekYou LLC in New Jersey Superior Court on April 30, 2025, asserting Daniel's Law claims on behalf of ~21,700 law enforcement officers, prosecutors, and other covered persons. Atlas and PeekYou entered a settlement agreement; the domain is now controlled by Atlas Data Privacy Corporation.

What replaced it: a domain notice explaining the settlement and pointing to the Superior Court of New Jersey. Same playbook as Radaris: Daniel's Law enforcement, broker by broker.

Source: peekyou.com — official domain-control notice

FAQ

Did Meta really delete an AI training clause from its terms?

Yes. On October 17, 2026, Meta removed a sentence from its Commercial Terms (effective October 30, 2026) that had allowed it to use content from business-linked domains to "provide, develop, improve, research, train, and maintain" its products. The removal followed publisher and legal backlash — Nieman Lab had legal experts read the original clause — and The Verge reported it the same day.

Can a company change its privacy policy without telling me?

For material changes, no — and the FTC enforces this. In June 2023 the FTC took action against genetic testing company 1Health.io (formerly Vitagene) for retroactively changing its privacy policy to broaden genetic-data sharing without notice or consent. The settlement: a $75,000 fine and destruction of retained DNA samples.

Does opting out of AI training delete data already used to train models?

No. Opt-outs are not retroactive. LinkedIn's terms are explicit that opting out of "Data for Generative AI Improvement" only stops future collection — anything gathered before the toggle stays in the training datasets. Once your data is in a model, you can't untrain it.

What happened to data brokers Radaris and PeekYou?

Both lost their domains to Atlas Data Privacy Corporation under New Jersey's Daniel's Law. Radaris.com was seized by a Superior Court order on August 27, 2026 after a default judgment; PeekYou's domain was taken over following a settled lawsuit filed April 30, 2025. Both domains now serve official transfer notices.
