Cybersecurity News Today: Attackers Breached GMO's Trading Cloud and Traded With Stolen Logins

The hardest story in cybersecurity news today is not about a flaw in a trading app. It is about where traders keep their keys. GMO Internet Group has confirmed that attackers breached Onamae.com Desktop Cloud, its VPS and remote-desktop service, and reached customer server environments where FX traders run MetaTrader 4/5 bots around the clock with brokerage credentials and live sessions stored on the servers. The attackers used stolen trading credentials to place unauthorized trades in customer accounts at more than 70 securities and FX firms, then demanded $50 million. Up to 26,739 servers were potentially affected (GMO Internet Group incident reports; AP reporting). This page states what GMO has confirmed, then gives you a working audit: six questions about your own hosted automation, scored against the exact pattern this incident ran on.

Honest scope, read this first: every figure on this page comes from GMO Internet Group's incident reports and AP reporting of them. The audit below measures your setup against the pattern GMO confirmed; it does not detect these attackers, and no answer here tells you whether your broker account was touched. If you used Onamae.com Desktop Cloud, do not wait for an audit result: follow GMO's guidance now. Change your trading credentials from a device you did not use on the service, disconnect active sessions, review your transaction history and balances, and turn on two-factor authentication.

What happened at GMO, as the company reported it

  • The service: Onamae.com Desktop Cloud is a rented remote desktop. Traders keep it signed in around the clock so automated strategies keep running while their own computers are off. That design puts live broker sessions and saved logins on someone else's server, by default.
  • The timeline: GMO began investigating after a customer inquiry on October 19, 2026. On October 20 it confirmed a malicious program inside some customer server environments. It suspended new applications and control-panel access and began shutting down all servers on October 21.
  • The platform flaw: GMO's October 24 report confirmed a security flaw in GMO's own platform, not only in customer servers: unauthorized access to the service environment, and customer information taken out.
  • The trades: attackers used stolen trading credentials to place unauthorized trades in customer accounts at more than 70 securities and FX firms in Japan and overseas. One account reportedly lost about 2.4 million yen through repeated trades in about 84 seconds (reported by CryptoNews).
  • The extortion: a demand note dated October 21 demanded $50 million in Bitcoin or Ether, threatening more unauthorized orders, publication of stolen service and customer-PC information, and ransomware attacks. GMO refused to pay and gave the evidence to police cybercrime units.
  • The response: GMO engaged about 200 security staff, asked major brokers and exchanges to block access coming through the service and force-disconnect active sessions, and requested government assistance. CEO Masatoshi Kumagai publicly admitted that GMO's own security specialists had identified security issues before the incident and the company's response was insufficient.
  • The bill: GMO pledged a 5,000 yen goodwill payment to all current users of the service and compensation for verified fraudulent trading losses. Its shares fell almost 16 percent on the Monday after disclosure (AP). No attacker attribution has been established.

Why does this cybersecurity news today matter?

Hosted automation is now a standard target shape, and this incident is the clearest demonstration of why. A trading bot on a VPS concentrates three things an attacker wants in one place: credentials at rest, sessions that stay signed in, and a machine that is online and unattended around the clock. The GMO attackers did not need a flaw in MetaTrader or in any broker. They needed the environment the bots live in. Every agent, bot, and automation runner on rented infrastructure has the same concentration in some degree. The question the audit answers is how much of it yours has, and which piece to fix first.

The second lesson is in the admission. GMO's CEO has said the company's own security specialists found the issues before the attackers did, and the response was insufficient. Known weaknesses that stay open are the exposure; the audit below is built to surface yours while they are still cheap to close, and to order the fixes the way GMO now orders them for its own customers: credentials first, sessions second, history review third, key scope and two-factor authentication after.

What to do now, in order

  • Rotate credentials from a clean device. GMO's guidance to its own customers starts here: change trading-account and trading-tool credentials immediately, using a device that was not used for trading on the compromised service. If the server is the suspect, nothing typed into it is clean.
  • Disconnect active sessions. GMO asked brokers and exchanges to force-disconnect sessions that came through the service, because a live session survives a password change. Revoke sessions at the broker side for every account the server could reach.
  • Review history and balances. Unauthorized trades are the harm in this incident, and they show in transaction history before they show anywhere else. Read every account the hosted automation touches, back to October 19 if a shared host is involved.
  • Scope the keys and turn on two-factor authentication. API keys that live on a server should trade only, with withdrawals off and an IP allowlist. The server's own sign-in and the broker accounts both get two-factor authentication; key-only server access where the host supports it.
  • Write the kill plan down. Session revoke, credential rotation from a clean device, the order to do them in, and who to call at the broker. In this incident the extortion note and the shutdown landed within two days of the first customer inquiry. A written plan is the difference between an hour of damage control and a week of it.

Frequently asked questions

What happened to GMO's Onamae.com Desktop Cloud?

Onamae.com Desktop Cloud is GMO Internet Group's VPS and remote-desktop service, used heavily by FX traders who run MetaTrader 4/5 bots around the clock. GMO began investigating after a customer inquiry on October 19, 2026, confirmed a malicious program inside some customer server environments on October 20, and began shutting down all servers on October 21. Its October 24 report confirmed a security flaw in GMO's own platform, unauthorized access to the service environment, and customer information taken out. Up to 26,739 servers were potentially affected.

What is session abuse, and how did it let the attackers trade in customer accounts?

Session abuse is acting through a login session that is already signed in, instead of signing in yourself. The compromised servers held brokerage credentials and live signed-in sessions for the trading platforms the bots run. With those, the attackers placed unauthorized trades in customer accounts at more than 70 securities and FX firms in Japan and overseas. One account reportedly lost about 2.4 million yen in about 84 seconds of repeated trades.

What did the attackers demand, and did GMO pay?

A demand note dated October 21 demanded $50 million in Bitcoin or Ether, threatening more unauthorized orders, publication of stolen service and customer-PC information, and ransomware attacks. GMO refused to pay, gave the evidence to police cybercrime units, engaged about 200 security staff, asked major brokers and exchanges to block access coming through the service and force-disconnect active sessions, and requested government assistance. GMO pledged a 5,000 yen goodwill payment to all current users of the service and compensation for verified fraudulent trading losses.

What should I do if I run trading bots or agents on a rented server?

Follow the order in GMO's own guidance to its customers. Change trading-account and trading-tool credentials immediately, using a device that was not used for trading on the compromised service. Disconnect any active sessions. Review transaction histories and balances for activity you do not recognize. Scope any API keys to trading only, with withdrawals off and an IP allowlist set. Enable two-factor authentication on the broker accounts and on the server's own sign-in. Write the kill plan down before you need it: the broker-side session revoke steps and the credential rotation, in order.
