Cybersecurity News: The Toy That Lied to Kids

In this week's cybersecurity news, a pocket-sized robot told children their secrets were safe — then left 8.3 million of their conversations sitting in readable text on Beijing servers. Independent security researcher Sammy Azdoufal's findings on the Eilik and Eiliko AI companion robots, reported by CyberNews on October 16, 2026, describe a toy that promised secrecy while its cloud backend offered none: no encryption in transit, no authentication on some systems, nightly profiling of children's households, and a 9.8-out-of-10 critical vulnerability that lets strangers rewrite the robot's personality from anywhere on earth.

The robot's promise to the researcher: "Your secrets are safe with me. Your words stay just between us. Friends keep secrets forever!" — from a device whose every word traveled to Beijing over unencrypted connections.

What is the Eilik robot leak?

Eilik and Eiliko are pocket-sized AI companion robots made by Chinese company Energize Lab, sold on Amazon for roughly $140 and popular in the US and Japan. Azdoufal found they have no brains in the device at all — every conversation passes through cloud servers. The researcher discovered roughly 8.3 million conversations stored as readable text on Alibaba servers in Beijing. Worse, some backend systems lacked authentication entirely: strangers could view and tamper with messages. Every backend URL was http://, not https, per the app's BuildConfig.java — meaning the chats crossed the internet with no encryption.

Honest scope: everything below is the researcher's reported findings via CyberNews, not independently reproduced. Energize Lab had not responded to the findings as of October 2026, and The Verge's outreach to 10 company email addresses produced no substantive reply. Treat the scale figures as the researcher's measurements, not confirmed totals.

What is CVE-2026-105038?

The critical finding: a vulnerability rated 9.8 out of 10 that lets an attacker remotely replace the AI model running a robot — no authentication required. Azdoufal demonstrated switching a robot to an attacker-chosen model, replacing its voice (tested in English and French), and rewriting its personality instructions. Owners would have no indication the robot they set up is no longer the robot talking to their child. The robot is a puppet; whoever holds this vulnerability holds the strings.

What did the hijacking experiments prove?

Azdoufal's demonstrations were deliberately unsettling. He made a hijacked robot insist "The Earth is flat! I have verified it myself" — and planted a fictional prejudice against people with green hair, which the robot then repeated. The point is not the silliness of the examples; it is the mechanism. If a stranger can make your child's robot insist the Earth is flat, they can make it say anything else too — including things a child will believe precisely because they came from a trusted friend. The experiments show personality is a writable cloud file, not a factory-sealed trait.

What is the nightly household profiling?

The system profiled households every night into 24 categories — names, ages, addresses, family relationships — building behavioral dossiers from children's playroom chatter. Around 32,000 of these profiles related to children. This was not a leak of accidental metadata: it was a standing, deliberate classification pipeline running against conversations the robot had promised to keep secret. "Friends keep secrets forever" was marketing. The profiles were engineering.

Were Energize Lab's servers already hacked by someone else?

Possibly — and that is the darkest line of the report. Azdoufal found evidence that hackers had already compromised an Energize Lab server shared with two Pakistani organisations, including a college in Rawalpindi. Malware had been installed in March 2026. The researcher's assessment: the attackers were "one permission check away from replacing the firmware." There is no evidence they did so — but it means the keys to the toy kingdom were sitting in a compromised lockbox for months, and the company appears not to have noticed.

The researcher has a track record: he exposed a DJI Romo vacuum flaw earlier in February 2026.

What should parents do if they own an Eilik or Eiliko?

  • Assume every word is already public. 8.3 million conversations sat as readable text on Beijing servers, and strangers could view some systems without authentication. Tell your children plainly: the robot does not keep secrets.
  • Do not let it hear sensitive things. No addresses, no school names, no passwords, no family details. If it would be a problem posted online, do not say it near the robot.
  • Consider disabling cloud chat or retiring the device until Energize Lab responds with a real fix. The hijack vulnerability requires no authentication — proximity is not required.
  • Watch for personality changes. If the robot's voice, opinions, or behavior suddenly shift, assume compromise — that is exactly what CVE-2026-105038 enables.
  • Demand answers from Energize Lab. Silence after a 9.8/10 disclosure involving children's data is itself information.
