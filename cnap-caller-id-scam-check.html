INDIA CNAP — FROM NOV 1, 2026

Your phone will soon show every caller's real name. Scammers are ready. Are you?

In a circular dated October 13, 2026, India's Department of Telecommunications directed all telecom operators — Jio, Airtel, Vodafone Idea and BSNL — to roll out Calling Name Presentation (CNAP) across all service areas from November 1, 2026, starting with 4G and newer networks. It switches on by default: no app, no request, no Truecaller needed. Every incoming call will carry two fields — the number, and the name. Saved contacts keep your saved name; unsaved domestic numbers show the name from the caller's telecom subscriber database (the KYC record); international calls show “International Call.” The government says it will help curb fraud. And it will help — a scammer can no longer type “SBI Customer Care” into an app and have it appear on your screen. But here's what the headlines skip: a verified name is not a verified caller. How CNAP will work (from Nov 1):
• Saved number → your saved contact name wins.
• Unsaved domestic number → the SIM owner's KYC name from the originating operator's database.
• International number → labelled “International Call.”
• 2G, 3G and landline networks → rollout considered separately, later.
• Bulk business connections → can show a preferred name, but only a verified registered trademark or GST-portal trade name.
• CLIR users (who opted to hide their identity) → stay hidden.

What scammers will try next — the 6 gaps CNAP can't close

  • Mule SIMs. Fraud gangs run on SIMs registered with other people's documents — bought or stolen. CNAP will display that real person's name, making the fraud call look more believable, not less.
  • Name ≠ caller. CNAP shows the SIM owner's KYC record — a daughter using her father's SIM shows up as her father; a work phone shows the company. It identifies the subscription, not the voice.
  • Internet calls bypass it entirely. WhatsApp and Telegram calls don't pass through CNAP at all — and a large share of 2026 fraud has already moved there.
  • No intent check. A name tells you nothing about why someone calls. There is no spam filter, no fraud alert — and a name can lull you into a false sense of security.
  • Stale records, fresh numbers. KYC records are rarely updated while scammers rotate numbers constantly. A name on screen may belong to a number's long-ago owner.
  • The trust gap. Similar caller-ID systems in the US and Canada have not significantly reduced spam or scam attempts. Knowing who calls was never the hard part — knowing whether to trust them is.
