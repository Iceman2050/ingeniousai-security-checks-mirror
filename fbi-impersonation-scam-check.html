SCAM CHECK

Is That "Federal Agent" Call Real?

Scammers are calling, texting, and video-calling Americans while pretending to be FBI agents — using spoofed FBI phone numbers, real agents' names, and even staged fake FBI offices on video. The FBI warned about it. Here's what the warning actually says, and a 5-question check: would you spot it?

What the FBI actually warned

The real warnings (not the hype)

  • Sept 29, 2026 — FBI Newark field office advisory: criminals are posing as FBI, DEA, and Secret Service agents. They spoof genuine agency phone numbers on caller ID and introduce themselves using actual federal agents' names. In one case, a scammer staged a video call with a suited man, a desk, and an FBI seal to fake a government office.
  • Sept 17, 2026 — FBI IC3 public service announcement: a national warning about criminals impersonating law enforcement and government officials — most often through unsolicited phone calls, but also texts and emails.
  • Scale: the FBI's 2025 Internet Crime Report recorded roughly 32,000 government-impersonation complaints and about $798 million in reported losses in a single year.

What the FBI says to do: find the agency's official number yourself (never use a number the caller gives you), call and explain you suspect fraud, and ask whether a real agent is contacting you. Report suspicious contacts at tips.fbi.gov or call 1-800-CALL-FBI. Preserve photos or recordings of fraudulent video meetings.

How the "federal investigation" scam works

  • First contact: an unsolicited call, text, or email claims you are under federal investigation, or that a warrant will be issued, or that your identity was used in a crime.
  • Borrowed credibility: your caller ID shows a real FBI office number. The caller gives a real agent's name — possibly one scraped from news or the web. If you call the office back and ask "does Agent X work there," they may say yes. That only proves the employee exists — not that they called you.
  • Urgency + secrecy: "act now or you'll be arrested," "don't tell anyone, this is a confidential investigation."
  • The payoff: you're told to pay to "clear" the case — gift cards, cryptocurrency, wire transfers, or cash. A real FBI agent will never demand payment over the phone or threaten arrest to collect money.
  • Video-call theater: some scammers now run staged video meetings with fake office backdrops to look official.

Why scammers may know real names

Context: the FBIJobs.gov breach

In late September 2026, the extortion group ShinyHunters claimed it breached the FBI's jobs portal (FBIJobs.gov) through an Oracle PeopleSoft system, saying it took personal data belonging to thousands of FBI employees. In early October, Reuters reported the FBI removed an Accenture contractor after its review blamed a missed security patch, and confirmed the breach exposed sensitive personal details of bureau employees.

Why this matters for you: stolen employee details make impersonation scams more convincing — a scammer with a real name, title, and phone format is harder to dismiss. This is reported context (ShinyHunters' claim + FBI's confirmed review), not a proven link to any specific scam call.

Red flags — the 30-second version

  • They contacted you first about a federal case.
  • Caller ID shows a government number — caller ID is trivially faked.
  • They know a real agent's name — names are public information.
  • Urgency, threats of arrest, demands for secrecy.
  • Payment by gift card, crypto, wire, or cash to "avoid arrest."
  • They discourage you from hanging up and verifying independently.

What to do if it happens to you

  1. Hang up. A real agent will not object. That is the whole test.
  2. Look up the number yourself. Type fbi.gov into your browser by hand and use the field-office directory — never use a number, link, or email from the suspicious message.
  3. Call and ask. Say someone contacted you claiming to be from the FBI and you want to confirm it.
  4. Check email domains character by character. Real federal email ends in .gov. Watch for near-misses like fbi-gov.com or fbi.gov.us.
  5. Report it: the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (ic3.gov), tips.fbi.gov, or 1-800-CALL-FBI. If you gave card details, call the fraud number on the back of your card.
