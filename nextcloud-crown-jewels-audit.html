What the attackers hunted

The people who exploited Nextcloud servers in September didn't browse around — they went straight for the crown jewels: KeePass databases, wallet.dat, password-manager artifacts. This is a defensive self-audit: find out what of yours lives anywhere the web-server user can read, before someone else inventories it for you. Nothing here attacks anything.

Tick anything that lives anywhere the web-server account (www-data) can read — home dirs, web roots, backup folders, /tmp. The meter counts your blast radius.

Find them fast (defensive commands)

find /var/www /home -name "*.kdbx" 2>/dev/null find /var/www /home -name "wallet.dat" 2>/dev/null find /var/www -name ".env" 2>/dev/null find /var/www -name "*.sql" -o -name "*.dump" 2>/dev/null

Run as your admin user. Anything these return that's readable by the web-server account belongs somewhere else — and if the server was vulnerable during Sept–Oct 2026, whatever it contained should be rotated.

What the reporting actually says

  • Attackers searched compromised servers for credential stores and crypto wallets — KeePass databases, wallet.dat, password-manager artifacts.
  • Reviewed requests showed discovery, not confirmed exfiltration — but anything readable by the web-server account was within reach.
  • No root access was reported. The blast radius is "everything www-data can read" — which is exactly what this audit maps.
  • Ransomware-operator exploitation reports are unconfirmed.
