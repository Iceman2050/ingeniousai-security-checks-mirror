Google SynthID Detector: What It Actually Tells You

On October 7, 2026, Google opened its SynthID Detector to the public at synthid.com — a free tool that checks whether an image, video, or audio file carries an invisible SynthID watermark. Upload a file, and within seconds you get a binary verdict: watermark detected or watermark not detected. The headlines called it a way to spot AI-generated media. The reality is narrower and more useful — as long as you understand what a "no" actually means.

What is the SynthID Detector?

The SynthID Detector is Google's public web tool for reading invisible watermarks that Google's AI models — and a handful of partners — embed into generated content at creation time. The watermark lives in the content itself: in the pixels of an image, the frequency components of audio, the frames of a video. It is imperceptible to people but machine-readable, and Google designed it to survive common edits like cropping, filters, frame-rate changes, and compression. An earlier version existed since 2025 for journalists and media professionals; the October 7 launch opened it globally in English to everyone, with sign-in via a Google, OpenAI, or Apple account.

The scale behind it is real: Google says it has watermarked more than 180 billion images and videos and the equivalent of 240,000 years of audio since SynthID launched in 2023. The same checks built into Google Search, the Gemini app, and Chrome already handle more than 1 million verification requests a day. As Pushmeet Kohli, VP of Science and Strategic Initiatives at Google DeepMind, put it: "With the new SynthID Detector, anyone can easily check if an image, video, or audio file was made with AI from Google or our partners, including OpenAI, NVIDIA, Kakao, and soon, Apple."

The big catch: a negative result says almost nothing

The central honest caveat. The SynthID Detector is a watermark reader, not a lie detector. Google's own FAQ draws the line in plain words: "This is not a general AI detector." Here is what each result actually means:

"Watermark detected" — strong evidence. The file came from one of the participating AI models. It means made or edited with an AI tool — the verdict can't tell the two apart, so a genuine photo retouched with an AI tool can come back positive. Never read a hit as "this is fake."

"Watermark not detected" — almost no information. The file could be human-made, or AI-made by any model outside the SynthID club: most open-weight models, Microsoft's and Meta's separate watermarking standards, or anything where heavy editing, aggressive recompression, or regeneration degraded the signal. Never cite a miss as proof a file is real. Every launch tester — from PCWorld to MUO, which ran viral memes and suspicious clips through it and got mostly negatives — reached the same conclusion: a clean result proves nothing either way.

How to use the synthid detector for real

  1. Upload the highest-quality original you have. A screenshot of a screenshot of an AI photo may not carry enough signal to detect. The watermark survives light compression but degrades under heavy transforms.
  2. Treat a hit as a lead, not a verdict. Positive = a participating model touched this file. Combine it with the source, context, and your eyes before drawing conclusions.
  3. Treat a miss as the absence of evidence, not evidence of absence. If it matters — a scam clip, a news tip, a defamation claim — keep investigating with other methods.
  4. Watch the free quota. Roughly ten checks per user per day, a limit Google says exists to stop anyone hammering the detector to train watermark-removal tools. Uploads are processed in real time and deleted right after; only a digital signature is kept for 24 hours to enforce the quota.
  5. Check the URL. Type synthid.com yourself — copycat "AI detector" sites will appear around a launch like this.

The EU AI Act context: why watermarking matters now

The launch did not happen in a vacuum. Since August 2, 2026, the EU AI Act has required machine-readable marking of AI-generated media — watermarks like SynthID are the compliance layer that rule points at. That is why coverage keeps expanding: the more providers that adopt compatible provenance technology, the more a tool like this can see. The industry-wide standard is still voluntary and fragmented — images, audio, and video have different technical requirements — which is exactly why the detector covers four partners and not forty.

Gemini can now flag AI-generated video segments

Alongside the detector launch, the Gemini app gained a related ability: upload a video (up to 100MB / 90 seconds) and it can tell you which segments of the clip are AI-generated, not just whether the file as a whole carries a mark. Segment-level attribution is the direction this is all heading — from "is this file AI?" to "which part of it is."

Frequently asked questions

Is Google's SynthID Detector free?

Yes. The SynthID Detector at synthid.com is free, with a cap of roughly ten checks per user per day. Sign-in with a Google, OpenAI, or Apple account is required.

Does a negative SynthID result mean a file is real?

No. A "watermark not detected" result only means no SynthID watermark was found. The file could be human-made, or AI-made by any model outside the SynthID system — most open-weight models, plus Microsoft's and Meta's own watermarking standards, are not covered.

Which AI tools does the SynthID Detector cover?

Google's own tools (Gemini, Nano Banana, Veo, Lyria, Flow, Imagen, ProducerAI, Vids) plus partner output from OpenAI, NVIDIA, and Kakao. Apple support is expected but not yet live.

Can the SynthID Detector tell if a whole file is AI-generated?

No — and it can't distinguish made-by-AI from edited-by-AI. Its verdict reads "made or edited with Google AI." A genuine photo retouched with an AI tool can come back positive.

Can watermarks be removed to fool the detector?

Heavy editing, aggressive recompression, and regeneration can weaken or strip the invisible signal, though Google designed it to survive common edits like cropping, filters, frame-rate changes, and compression. No watermark signal is completely robust.
