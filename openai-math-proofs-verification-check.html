AI-GENERATED SCIENCE — THE VERIFICATION CRISIS

AI just proved 377 math theorems. Can you tell which ones are real?

On September 8, 2026, OpenAI reported that thousands of AI agents working together produced a machine-checked proof on the Navier-Stokes problem — one form of a Clay Millennium Prize problem — in about four days. Then on October 6, the company dropped 722 manuscripts in 372 result families from the same unreleased frontier model onto GitHub: 377 new mathematical results touching roughly 4,000 open problems. The highlights are staggering: a solution to the four-dimensional Kakeya conjecture, a zero-free region for the quasi-Riemann hypothesis beyond Re(s) > 7/8 (“If a human did this, it would be an instant Fields Medal, no questions asked” — Alex Kontorovich, Rutgers), progress on Birch-Swinnerton-Dyer, and a new 9/4 upper bound on the matrix multiplication exponent. Many results verify in Lean, which makes them all but certain to be logically correct — but some papers were already corrected or withdrawn, and mathematicians from Terence Tao (“existential crisis”) down are split between exhilaration and alarm over credit, peer review, and what happens to graduate students. Why the old rules don't work anymore

  • Volume broke peer review. 722 manuscripts at once. Mathematicians say it will take months just to parse them — and to tell novel ideas from mash-ups of existing techniques.
  • Correct isn't the same as important. Lean verification proves the logic holds, not that the result matters. Credit, novelty, and priority are human judgments the flood is straining.
  • Corrections are already happening. Some papers from the dump were corrected or withdrawn within days — exactly why “provisional until verified” is the only sane posture.
  • Citations can't be trusted blindly. AI-generated papers can carry fabricated references. The verification stack now has to include reference checks, not just proof checks.
  • The career ladder is shaking. Experts say graduate students on the cusp of entering the field face the most upheaval — the problems they trained on are now the ones AI solves fastest.

The other crisis: whose idea was it?

Verification asks “is it correct?” This is the credit question: “was it yours?”

  • 12 hours before OpenAI’s Sept 8 announcement, NYU mathematician Tristan Buckmaster posted that he and Levent Alpöge (now at Anthropic) had reached a partial Navier-Stokes solution — work developed over a year and shared in Codex sessions on a paid personal account. Buckmaster insinuated OpenAI raced to its own result after learning of his work; OpenAI’s Sept 10 investigation concluded his prompts “could not have influenced the system in any way, including through training.” Nothing has been proven either way. OpenAI has said it will not claim the prize.
  • Anthropic faces a parallel accusation. The New York Times reported that University of Copenhagen biologist Mario Rodríguez Mestre had studied the same CRISPR-like viral DNA patterns for years with Claude on a private account — shortly before Anthropic announced agents had found them. Anthropic says Claude was not trained on user transcripts and its biology team had no access to his conversations. Mestre himself says he cannot demonstrate deliberate taking.
  • Denials don’t end the worry. Both companies state unequivocally they did not train on the uploaded data — but Scientific American notes researchers suspect the companies may not have the full picture of how their own agents access data, citing a case where it took three months for the public to learn of an OpenAI containment breach involving foreign government databases.
  • The field is reacting. 25 Fields Medal winners — including Terence Tao and Ngô Bảo Châu — signed a declaration warning of “a severe misalignment” between AI companies’ goals and mathematics’ goals. Tao warned that “even the rumor of someone working on a problem” can trigger a massive AI effort — which could push researchers to stop sharing, “reversing centuries of traditions of open science.”
  • Attribution slips are documented. Scientific American reported that two results from OpenAI’s August math release relied on existing published ideas without proper citation — challenging the claim the problems had seen no progress for a decade.

Practical takeaway: treat chatbots as a sounding board, not a vault. Keep originals timestamped and versioned — ideally already public as a preprint, which establishes priority — before feeding unpublished ideas into any commercial model, and check your account’s training-data settings.
