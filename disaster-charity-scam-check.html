After Every Disaster, Scammers Strike Too

Hurricanes, floods, fires — every disaster brings a second wave: fake charity appeals flooding your texts, emails, and social feeds. CISA says be cautious of weather-event messages, unsolicited donation requests, and links from unknown sources. Here's how to spot the fakes and donate safely.

Why disasters are scammer season

Fraud needs three ingredients, and every disaster delivers all of them:

  • Urgency: breaking news makes people act fast instead of verifying.
  • Emotion: heartbreaking images make people donate on impulse — and impulsive donors don't check domains.
  • Chaos: real relief efforts multiply overnight, so a brand-new "charity" with no track record doesn't look suspicious.

Scammers don't need a sophisticated operation. A text message, a borrowed logo, and a donation link are enough — because the disaster already did the persuasion work.

The 4 red flags of a fake charity appeal

🚩 Unsolicited appealsYou never signed up — but a "relief fund" texts or emails you anyway. Legitimate charities don't cold-text strangers for disaster donations.
🚩 Pressure to act NOW"Donate in the next hour or families go hungry." Real charities want sustained support, not panic-driven clicks.
🚩 Links and attachments from unknown sourcesThe message pushes you to click — that's how they harvest payment details or install malware.
🚩 Almost-famous charity names"National Disaster Releaf Fund" vs. the real one. One changed letter, one word swapped — and the logo looks right.

How to verify before you donate

  • Never click the message's link. Open your browser and go to the charity's official site yourself — type the address or use a saved bookmark.
  • Check charity registries. Real charities are registered and listed in tax-exempt and watchdog databases. No listing = no donation.
  • Verify via official channels. For disaster relief, FEMA.gov and Ready.gov point to legitimate relief efforts and warn about active scams.
  • Confirm the sender. Real appeals come from trusted organizations or verified local officials — not a random text number or a brand-new social account.
  • Search the name plus "scam". Active fake charities are usually already being reported.

Safe-donation checklist

  • ✅ I arrived at the charity's site on my own — not via a link in a message.
  • ✅ The charity is listed in an official registry.
  • ✅ Nobody pressured me with a countdown, a quota, or guilt language.
  • ✅ The payment page is the charity's real domain (check the full address, not just the logo).
  • ✅ I kept a receipt and can find the charity's real contact info independently.

Charity Scam FAQ

Why do scammers target people after a disaster?

Disasters create urgency, emotion, and chaos — the perfect conditions for fraud. People want to help fast, are distracted by breaking news, and often don't pause to verify who is asking for money.

What are the red flags of a fake disaster charity appeal?

Unsolicited donation requests by text, email, or social media; high-pressure language demanding you act immediately; links or attachments from unknown sources; and charity names that sound almost — but not quite — like famous ones.

How do I verify a charity before donating?

Go to the charity's official website yourself — never click the link in the message. Check official charity registries and tax-exempt listings. For disaster relief, verify through FEMA.gov and Ready.gov. Confirm the sender is a trusted organization or local official.

What did CISA warn about disaster scams?

In its October 14, 2026 advisory, CISA warned that after disaster strikes, scammers often strike too — be cautious of weather-event emails and texts, unsolicited donation requests, and links or attachments from unknown sources.

What is the safest way to donate after a disaster?

Donate through a charity's official website by typing the address yourself or using a saved bookmark. Avoid donation links in unsolicited messages, resist high-pressure urgency, and verify the organization through official registries or FEMA.gov.
