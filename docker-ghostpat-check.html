CVE-2026-105445 “GHOSTPAT” — CVSS 9.4 CRITICAL — FIXED SEP 30, 2026

Someone could have published Docker images under your name

For over two years, a Docker Hub flaw called GhostPat let an attacker push, pull, or delete images as you — by creating an account, minting a personal access token, converting the account into an organization, and naming you as its owner. The token kept its secret and silently became yours at the registry. Docker fixed it on September 30 (under four hours after the report) and found no evidence of exploitation across ~304,000 reviewed pushes — but trust Docker's audit and verify. The 60-second check

1

Check for Docker's email — on October 2, Docker emailed every user whose account received a transferred token, plus the org owners involved. Search your inbox for Docker Hub security notices from early October:

Got the email → you were in the blast radius. Continue through all steps below — Docker deactivated the transferred tokens on Oct 8, but verify your repos yourself.
No email → Docker's review didn't flag your account. Still worth the 60 seconds — the audit below is good hygiene regardless.
2

Audit your personal access tokens — hub.docker.com → Account Settings → Personal Access Tokens. Each token acts as your account at the registry with the permissions it was created with:

  • Delete anything you don't recognize — a token you didn't create is the whole attack.
  • Delete anything unused — dead tokens are pure risk surface.
  • Rotate what's left — especially tokens created before October 2026.
3

Review your repositories for phantom pushes — open each of your repos on Docker Hub and check the tags:

A tag you didn't push, or a push timestamp from a session you don't remember → investigate immediately. An attacker with push access could have trojanized an image under your trusted name — the supply-chain nightmare scenario.
Every tag accounted for → good. Your images are yours.
4

Lock it down going forward:

  • Enable two-factor authentication on Docker Hub if you haven't.
  • Scope every token to least privilege — read-only where possible; short expiries where the UI allows.
  • Never accept an unexpected organization-owner designation — Docker now requires owner consent for conversions (part of the fix), so a consent prompt you didn't initiate is a red flag.

Nothing to install or patch — the fix was server-side on Docker Hub itself. Your job is verification + hygiene.

How the attack worked (so you know what you're checking for)

  • Docker Hub can't move a repo between accounts — instead you convert a personal account into an organization keeping the same name, and name an existing account as owner.
  • The bug: conversion transferred the account's PATs to the new owner without changing the token secret and without notifying them. The token's creator could still use it.
  • Attacker's recipe: create account → mint PAT → convert to org naming the victim as owner → the token now authenticates as the victim at the registry.
  • Blast radius was registry-only: push/pull/delete within the token's scope. No web-UI access, no password/email/2FA changes, no full account takeover.
  • Found by Peter Molnar and Krisztian Hozak (mptrdev.com); Docker shipped the fix under four hours after the report. Tokens are no longer transferred on conversion, and owner designation now needs consent.
