How the tamper window worked

Normal operation — AOMEI Backupper clients poll the update server. Server responds with the legitimate updater. Everything signed, everything trusted.
The window opens — 5 to 10 minutes — attackers swap in a backdoored updater on the vendor's own server. The channel is legitimate. The signature may even still look right.
Anyone polling inside the window — downloads the backdoor — from a server they trust, through a process their firewall allows, with no phishing involved.
The window closes — the legitimate updater is restored. Forensic traces on the server are minimal. Clients that polled five minutes later saw nothing wrong.

The scary arithmetic: if your fleet of 500 machines all polls at boot every morning, the chance that someone lands inside a 10-minute window is effectively 100%.

Frequently asked questions

What is the UNC7017 attack?

UNC7017 is the threat-actor designation for a campaign disclosed October 16, 2026 by Google Threat Intelligence Group and Mandiant. From May to July 2026, the attackers briefly tampered with AOMEI Backupper's own update servers in 5–10-minute windows; users whose clients checked for updates inside a window downloaded a backdoored updater instead of the legitimate one.

Was AOMEI hacked?

AOMEI's own update servers were tampered with during the UNC7017 campaign — but only in brief 5–10-minute windows between May and July 2026, according to Google Threat Intelligence Group and Mandiant (disclosed Oct 16, 2026). Clients polling for updates inside a window received a backdoored updater. AOMEI itself, and clients that updated outside the windows, were not affected.

What is a tamper window attack?

A tamper window attack is a supply-chain technique where attackers compromise a legitimate vendor's update or distribution infrastructure for a very short time — here 5–10 minutes — then remove the malicious content before anyone notices. Anyone who checks for updates inside that window receives malware from a server they trust; everyone else sees nothing wrong. It defeats reputation-based trust because the malicious file comes from the real, signed update channel.

How do I know if I downloaded the UNC7017 backdoor?

The campaign ran May–July 2026, so the key question is whether your AOMEI Backupper checked for updates or downloaded one in that period. Look for update activity in those months, verify the digital signature of installed components against AOMEI's published certificates, and run a current antivirus/EDR scan. If in doubt, reinstall from a fresh download obtained directly from the vendor and verify the signature.

Can I still trust automatic software updates?

Yes — disabling auto-updates leaves you exposed to the far larger set of known, unpatched vulnerabilities. The UNC7017 lesson is to harden the update channel instead: verify code signatures, stage updates on a pilot group first, monitor update traffic for anomalies, and keep backups offline.
