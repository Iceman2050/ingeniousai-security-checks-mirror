What is a deepfake voice scam?

A deepfake voice scam uses AI to clone someone's voice — often a family member — then calls targets with urgent fake emergencies to steal money. Seconds of social media audio is enough to build a convincing clone.

It sounds like your grandson. It sounds like your daughter. It is not. These are deepfake voice scams — AI-cloned voices used to fake family emergencies and demand money. Step 1 — Generate your family code word

A code word is the one defense a voice clone cannot beat: a secret decided in person, asked on any panicked call. Generate one now, then tell every family member face-to-face. Never text it. Never email it.

The one-page protocol

  1. Who knows it: every family member, decided and spoken in person. If someone was not there, they do not have it — brief them directly, never over text or email.
  2. When to use it: any caller — no matter how familiar the voice — who claims an emergency and wants money, information, or secrecy. Ask: "What's our code word?"
  3. If they cannot answer: the call is over. Hang up immediately. Then call the real person on a phone number you already have saved — never the number the caller gives you. If you cannot reach them, call another relative.
  4. Never do on that call: no wire transfers, no crypto, no gift cards, no OTP or PIN codes. A real emergency survives ten minutes of verification.

Why a code word, really?


The scam the FTC describes works because love is faster than doubt: a panicked voice that sounds like your grandson, an arrest or a wreck, money needed right now, and — critically — do not tell anyone else. Every element is engineered to stop you verifying. A code word agreed in advance breaks the spell in one question, because no cloning tool can guess a secret it has never heard. FAQ — deepfake family protection
How do AI voice cloning scams work?
Scammers clone a loved one's voice from a short audio clip, then call pretending there is an emergency — an accident, an arrest, a kidnapping — and pressure the victim to send money or share sensitive information. The FTC warns the clone can sound exactly like the real person, so the voice alone is never proof of identity.
Can scammers clone your voice from social media?
Yes. A short clip from TikTok, Facebook, Instagram, or YouTube — or even a voicemail greeting — can be enough material for a convincing voice clone. Limit how much of your voice is public, set profiles to private, and treat any public audio as potential cloning material.
What is the best way to protect your family from AI voice scams?
Create a family verification plan before a scam happens: agree on a private family code word decided in person and never shared by text or email, never send money based on one incoming call alone, and always hang up and call back on a saved number. Use the generator and test above to practice.
Can you detect a deepfake phone call by listening?
Not reliably. There may be small tells — odd pauses or unnatural phrasing — but AI voices are now close enough that listening is not a safe test. The defense is verification: hang up, call back on a saved number, and ask for your family's code word.
What should you do if you receive an AI voice cloning scam call?
Secrecy plus urgency plus a hard-to-reverse payment (wire, crypto, gift cards) is the classic scam pattern. Do not send anything on that call. Hang up, verify the story through a separate channel, and report the attempt to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.


What's the fastest way to stop a deepfake voice scam mid-call?


Hang up immediately — even if the voice sounds exactly like your loved one — then call them back on a number you already have saved. Ask for your family's pre-arranged code word; scammers can't guess it. Never send money during that first call, and report the attempt to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
