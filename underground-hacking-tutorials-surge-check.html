Underground Hacking Tutorials Doubled: What Radware Found

The criminal classroom is open and enrollment is surging. Radware's research into underground hacking forums found that new tutorial output roughly doubled over the past year — and the curriculum has shifted hard toward direct financial fraud. The researchers analyzed 8,870 tutorial posts across 24 deep- and dark-web forums published between December 2022 and April 2026, and after removing reposts, the dataset held 3,034 unique hacking and fraud guides. The headline finding: the forums are no longer just recycling old tricks. They are producing new ones — and your phone number and social accounts are the prime targets.

What is an underground hacking tutorial?

An underground hacking tutorial is a how-to guide published on deep- or dark-web forums that teaches criminal techniques — card theft and cash-out, phishing, account takeover, offensive security methods. Radware's study, reported by Help Net Security on July 14, 2026, treated these tutorials as a dataset: 8,870 posts narrowed to 3,034 unique guides after deduplication, tracked over more than three years. The point of studying them is not to learn the crimes but to see where criminals are investing their teaching effort — because what gets taught today gets attempted tomorrow.

The shift: carding is now the biggest subject

The subject mix changed as output grew. Carding — the theft and fraudulent use of payment card data, plus the cash-out techniques that convert stolen data into money — grew from 19% of tutorials in 2024 to 38% in 2026, making it the largest category in the dataset. Over the same period, black-hat SEO and affiliate manipulation fell from 33% to 13%. Offensive security and phishing also gained share.

The direction is unmistakable: away from traffic manipulation and toward direct financial fraud. And the guides are getting more complete — many now teach multiple stages of fraud in one document, covering both the techniques used to gain access to accounts and the methods used to monetize stolen credentials. By teaching the break-in and cash-out sides side by side, tutorial authors are giving readers the complete playbook, not just a few random tricks.

Who they target: your phone number and your social accounts

Of the tutorials that named a specific victim brand or platform, telecom and social media were the most frequently referenced sectors — together accounting for nearly two-thirds of industry-tagged content. The report links that focus to the role these services play in fraud operations:

  • Intercepting one-time passcodes — your phone number receives the 2FA codes that guard your bank, email, and crypto accounts. That is why SIM-swap and number-porting attacks exist.
  • Obtaining verified accounts — social accounts carry identity, reputation, and a trusted network of contacts, making them valuable for further fraud.
  • Supporting cash-out activity — compromised accounts are the laundering rails that turn stolen data into spendable money.

This is the part that reaches past corporate security teams and into your pocket: the underground curriculum is explicitly organized around the two things most people carry everywhere — a phone number and a social identity.

AI is lowering the bar for tutorial authors

Radware found that AI is making it easier to produce convincing underground tutorials. Two of the three case studies in the report involved AI-assisted content creation or repackaging — including an AI-generated catalog of attack techniques whose author used hidden content and engagement requirements to build reputation within a hacking forum, and public security research rewritten and republished under a threat actor's name to manufacture false authority. Researchers say newer agentic AI tools could narrow the remaining gap, since many fraud guides still depend on the precise sequence and timing needed to exploit business logic in online applications.

There is a reputation economy underneath all of this, too. Radware found that among tutorials reposted at least 10 times, 79% were distributed through coordinated campaigns run by a single account or hidden premium members — repost volume is as much a measure of promotion as of community interest, with authors publishing to build credibility or to promote paid criminal services.

What this means for you: the SIM-swap and account-takeover angle

When the underground's biggest growth category is carding and its most-targeted sectors are telecom and social media, the defensive lesson is personal, not just corporate. Your phone number is an attack surface. Practical steps:

  • Move 2FA off SMS to an authenticator app or hardware key wherever offered — SMS codes are exactly what the telecom-targeted tutorials are after.
  • Lock your number with your carrier: set a port-out PIN or number-transfer lock so your number cannot be moved to an attacker's SIM.
  • Harden your social accounts with non-SMS 2FA and review connected apps and sessions regularly — a compromised account becomes a fraud tool.
  • Watch for the setup: unexpected "verify your number" prompts, carrier texts about SIM changes you did not request, or password-reset emails you did not trigger.
  • Separate your identity anchors: do not use the same phone number and recovery email for banking, social, and crypto — compartmentalize.
