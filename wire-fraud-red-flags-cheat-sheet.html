Wire Fraud Red Flags Cheat Sheet — what is phishing with a bank account at the end of it

Reviewed October 25, 2026 — Ingenious AI security checks.

Direct answer: Wire fraud is the payment crime that usually starts as phishing: a convincing email changes the bank details on a real invoice, and the money wires to the fraudster. Key facts

  • The fraud is a detail change: the invoice, the supplier and the amount are real — only the destination account is the fraudster's.
  • 3 ceremony steps defeat it: a mandatory hold, an out-of-band callback to the number on file, and dual approval on separate devices.
  • Urgency is the tell: real suppliers survive a callback; fraud instructs you, specifically, not to call.

What is phishing when it ends in a wire transfer?

It is business email compromise aimed at the payment itself: attackers phish or spoof their way into a real invoicing thread, then substitute new bank details at the moment of payment. The request looks routine because almost everything in it is genuine — the fraud lives in one changed field, which is why reading the email harder never works and verifying out-of-band always does.

  • Changed bank details on an otherwise normal invoice or payment instruction — the single highest-risk signal in this entire cheat sheet.
  • Urgency and secrecy: 'pay today, do not call the office, the director is travelling'.
  • Lookalike sender domains: one letter off your real supplier or executive domain (see the Domain Impersonation cheat sheet).
  • Thread hijack: the request continues a genuine thread but quietly moves replies to a controlled address.
  • New beneficiary plus round pressure: first-time payee, unusual amount or split payments designed to dodge approval thresholds.
  • Verification resistance: any instruction that discourages the callback is the fraud introducing itself.

What is the payment-verification ceremony?

  1. Mandatory hold: new or changed wire instructions wait a defined period before they can be paid — speed is the fraud's weapon, so the ceremony removes it.
  2. Out-of-band callback: call the number already on file for the supplier — never a number from the requesting email — and confirm the details voice-to-voice.
  3. Dual approval on separate devices: a second approver, on their own device, confirms amount, beneficiary and the callback result.
  4. Hash-chained audit ledger: every hold, callback and approval is recorded in a tamper-evident ledger, so the ceremony itself can be proven later.

The ceremony feels slow exactly once — the first time. After that it is simply how wires are sent, the way a seatbelt is simply how cars are driven.

Frequently asked questions

What is phishing in wire fraud?

In wire fraud, phishing is the delivery mechanism: a spoofed or compromised email account inserts fraudulent bank details into a genuine payment relationship. The invoice and amount are usually real, which is why the defence is not spotting the fake email but verifying the details out-of-band before any money moves.

What are the biggest wire fraud red flags?

Changed bank details on a known supplier, urgency paired with secrecy, lookalike sender domains, replies quietly rerouted to a new address, first-time beneficiaries under time pressure, and any instruction that discourages calling to verify. One flag means run the ceremony carefully; changed details plus verification resistance means stop entirely.

What is a payment-verification ceremony?

It is the fixed sequence every wire passes before release: a mandatory hold on new or changed instructions, an out-of-band callback to the number on file to confirm details, dual approval by two people on separate devices, and a hash-chained audit record of each step.
