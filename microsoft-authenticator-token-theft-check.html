Cybersecurity News Today: Authenticator Flaw Let Any App Pose as Teams and Take Work Tokens

The sharpest story in cybersecurity news today is about a guard who checked the wrong ID. Microsoft has fixed a flaw in Microsoft Authenticator for Android, tracked as CVE-2026-80097 and rated 8.6 (Important), that let a malicious app with no permissions pose as a trusted Microsoft app such as Teams and collect sign-in tokens for the victim's work account (The Hacker News, October 27, 2026, reporting the research and Microsoft's advisory). In the researchers' tests, the tokens gave access to the account's email, files, and calendars. This page states what Microsoft and the researchers have published, then gives you a working audit: six questions about your own phone and work setup, scored against the exact pattern this flaw ran on.

Honest scope, read this first: the audit below measures your setup against the pattern in CVE-2026-80097. It does not scan your phone, and no answer here tells you whether a token was taken from your account. Two things the sources leave open, stated plainly. Microsoft's advisory does not say whether updating revokes tokens a malicious app may already have obtained. And the sources disagree on one point: Enclave reported tokens returned with no user interaction beyond a signed-in account, while Microsoft's advisory says the user must complete an authentication flow. Both positions are reported on this page, attributed.

What happened, as Microsoft and Enclave reported it

  • The broker role: on Android, Authenticator does more than show six-digit codes. It acts as a broker. When Teams, Outlook, or another Microsoft app needs to sign a user in, it asks Authenticator, and Authenticator hands back a token. The broker controls access to the whole account, so which app it grants a token to is the decision that matters.
  • The open door: Authenticator exposes a component, an activity named AccountChooserActivity, that any app on the device can start. When an attacker's app started it, Authenticator built its token request from values the attacker's app supplied, including the name of the app it claimed to be. The app said it was Teams, and the broker believed it (Enclave's technical write-up, October 23, 2026).
  • The check that checked the wrong thing: a signature check was supposed to stop this. The check looked up the real signature of the app being impersonated and confirmed the request matched it. Because Teams was genuinely installed on the device, its real signature was there to read, so the check passed. Authenticator could also see which app had actually made the request, Enclave found, but it did not use that to decide whether to hand over a token.
  • The result: in Enclave's tests, posing as Teams and requesting Microsoft Graph tokens returned permissions to read and send mail, read and write files, and read and write calendars, among others. The tokens apply to the work account signed into Authenticator on that device.
  • The researcher: the research comes from Ofek Levin, working through a fellowship program at the security firm Enclave, which published the technical details on October 23, 2026.
  • The rating and the fix: Microsoft assigned the CVE on September 8, 2026, rated it 8.6 on the CVSS scale and labeled it Important. The fix shipped in the September 2026 Patch Tuesday. The fixed version is Authenticator for Android 6.2606.4246; update to that version or later.
  • The disagreement: Enclave reported that its proof of concept returned tokens without any user interaction, other than the presence of a signed-in account. Microsoft's advisory says exploitation requires installing a malicious app on the device and convincing the user to complete an authentication flow in Authenticator, and its CVSS score reflects user interaction.
  • Exploitation status: Microsoft says the flaw has not been used in attacks and was not publicly disclosed before the fix, and rates exploitation as less likely. The flaw is not listed in the U.S. CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog as of October 26, 2026 (The Hacker News).

Put the shape in proportion. The cryptography held. The signature was genuine, and a signature compared with itself matches 100 percent of the time. What failed was the question the broker asked: it verified that Teams is Teams, when the decision needed which app is asking. Any system that hands out tokens, keys, or sessions on the strength of a claimed identity has the same failure available to it. The audit below scores how much of that shape your own setup carries.

Why does this cybersecurity news today matter?

The broker pattern is everywhere now, and phones are only the most visible case. An authenticator broker, an identity provider, an agent gateway: each one sits between a caller and an account, and each one decides who gets a token. CVE-2026-80097 is a clean demonstration that a verification step can run exactly as coded and still protect nothing, because it verified the claim instead of the caller. The fix on the defender side is a habit, not a patch: bind every token decision to the identity of the caller that the platform itself reports, never to a name the caller supplies.

The second lesson is about the installed app next door. The attack needed no permissions and no exploit chain. It needed one unvetted app on the same phone as a work account, plus a broker willing to trust it. That is why Microsoft guidance pairs the update with device management: require the fixed version, and know which apps share a device with your work accounts.

What to do now, in order

  • Update Authenticator. Bring Microsoft Authenticator for Android to version 6.2606.4246 or later from the Play Store, then confirm the version on the app's About screen. This is Microsoft's fix, and it is the first step for every reader with a work account on Android.
  • Enforce the minimum version. Organizations that manage Android devices can require that minimum version through device management. A fix that depends on every employee updating by hand is a fix that stays partial.
  • Review the app inventory. On any phone that holds a work account, list the installed apps and remove what you do not recognize or need. The malicious app in this pattern needed no permissions, so the permissions screen is not where you will spot it.
  • Revoke sessions where compromise is a concern. Microsoft's advisory does not say whether updating cancels tokens a malicious app may already have obtained. Our recommendation: revoke the account's sessions from the account security page, then sign back in. It costs minutes and it ends the value of any token already taken.

Frequently asked questions

What is CVE-2026-80097?

CVE-2026-80097 is a flaw in Microsoft Authenticator for Android, assigned on September 8, 2026 and rated 8.6 (Important) by Microsoft. On Android, Authenticator also works as a sign-in broker: apps such as Teams and Outlook ask it for sign-in tokens. Authenticator exposed an activity, AccountChooserActivity, that any app on the device could start with no permissions. It built the token request from values the calling app supplied, including the identity the app claimed to be. A malicious app could claim to be Teams and receive Microsoft Graph tokens for the work account signed into Authenticator on that device. Microsoft fixed the flaw in the September 2026 Patch Tuesday; the fixed version is Authenticator for Android 6.2606.4246.

Why did the signature check fail to stop the impersonation?

A signature check existed, but it verified the wrong identity. It looked up the real signature of the app being impersonated and confirmed the request matched it. Because Teams was genuinely installed on the device, its real signature was there to read, so the check passed. Authenticator could also see which app had actually made the request, the Enclave research found, but it did not use that to decide whether to hand over a token. The broker verified the claim instead of the caller.

Do the researchers and Microsoft agree on how the attack works?

They agree on the flaw and disagree on one point. Enclave reported that its proof of concept returned tokens without any user interaction, other than the presence of a signed-in account. Microsoft's advisory says exploitation requires installing a malicious app on the device and convincing the user to complete an authentication flow in Authenticator, and its CVSS score reflects user interaction. Microsoft also says the flaw has not been used in attacks, was not publicly disclosed before the fix, and is not listed in the CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog as of October 26, 2026.
