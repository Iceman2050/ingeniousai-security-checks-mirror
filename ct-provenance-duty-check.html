What is the Connecticut AI provenance duty?

The Connecticut AI provenance duty is a statutory requirement for organizations deploying AI to maintain records proving the provenance of AI-generated content — what the AI made, when, and under whose control.

Since October 1, 2026, Connecticut has required large consumer-facing AI providers to embed provenance data — watermarks, C2PA-style metadata — in the AI-generated or AI-altered audio, images, and video they put out. It's the first US state AI-content provenance duty, and only the state Attorney General enforces it. Six questions sort you into COMPLIANT, EXPOSED, or EXEMPT.

Short answer: §15 of Public Act 26-15 applies to covered providers — anyone who creates, codes, or produces a generative AI system with more than 1 million monthly users, publicly accessible to consumers for personal use, that generates images, audio, or video. Text-only systems, internal-only tools, and government agencies are out of scope. If you're in scope, provenance data must be embedded to the extent commercially and technically reasonable, and it must be hard to strip.

What the statute actually requires — §15 in plain terms

ElementWhat it says
Who is coveredA "covered provider": any person who creates, codes, or otherwise produces a generative AI system with more than 1 million users per month that is publicly accessible to consumers for personal use. Government agencies are excluded.
What contentAudio, image, and video (or combinations) that the system creates or materially alters. Text is not covered.
The dutyTo the extent commercially and technically reasonable, include provenance data so a consumer can assess whether the content was created or materially altered by the provider's system.
The standardUse commercially and technically reasonable methods — including the relevant C2PA standards — to make the provenance data difficult to tamper with, remove, or disassociate from the content.
Not requiredNo identified-individual PII in the provenance data; no disclosure of trade secrets or confidential/proprietary design information.
Excluded contentVideo games and interactive experiences; technical functions like noise reduction. Minor edits are not "materially altered": brightness, contrast, color, sharpening, saturation, filters, resizing, scaling, cropping, format conversion, resampling, denoising, background-noise removal.
EnforcementConnecticut Attorney General only, under the Connecticut Unfair Trade Practices Act. No private right of action.

Important scope note: this duty applies to the provider of the AI system — the company that built it — not to an end user who merely posts AI-generated content. If you don't build or operate the generative system, you're not a covered provider.

Frequently asked questions — FAQ

What is Connecticut's AI provenance duty?

Section 15 of Connecticut Public Act 26-15 ("An Act Concerning Online Safety", also called the CART Act — Substitute Senate Bill No. 5, signed May 27, 2026). It requires covered providers to embed provenance data in AI-created or materially altered audio, image, and video content, to the extent commercially and technically reasonable. It took effect October 1, 2026, and is the first US state-level AI-content provenance duty.

Who does the Connecticut provenance duty apply to?

"Covered providers": any person who creates, codes, or otherwise produces a generative AI system that has more than one million users per month and is publicly accessible to consumers for personal use. The system must generate images, audio, or video. Federal, state, and local government agencies are excluded.

What must covered providers actually do?

To the extent commercially and technically reasonable: (1) include provenance data in any audio, image, or video content their system creates or materially alters, in a manner that lets a consumer assess whether the content was AI-created or AI-altered; and (2) use methods — including the relevant Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA) standards — that make the provenance data difficult to tamper with, remove, or disassociate from the content.

Does the Connecticut law cover AI-generated text?

No. Section 15 covers audio, image, and video content only. Text-only generative systems are outside the duty.

Who enforces the Connecticut provenance duty?

Only the Connecticut Attorney General, under the Connecticut Unfair Trade Practices Act. There is no private right of action.

What counts as materially altered content?

A substantial alteration of the data in the content. The statute excludes minor modifications that do not significantly change the perceived content or meaning: changes in brightness, contrast or color, sharpening, saturation, application of a filter, resizing, scaling, cropping, format conversion, resampling, denoising, or removal of background noise in audio.
